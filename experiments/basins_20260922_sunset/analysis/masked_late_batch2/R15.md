## Hop 10

On the morning the fishing boats stayed in the harbor, Tomas walked down to the lighthouse where his aunt Mirela had kept the lamp burning for thirty-one years. The tower stood at the end of a long stone jetty, its white paint flaking in the salt wind, and its spiral staircase had exactly one hundred and twelve steps, each worn smooth in the middle by generations of careful feet. Mirela was already at the top, polishing the great glass lens with a soft cloth, and she handed him a second cloth without a word. Together they cleaned every prism until the gray light of the coming storm broke into small rainbows across the walls. She showed him how to trim the wick, how to wind the clockwork that turned the lens, and how to read the barometer whose needle sank lower by the hour. As the waves grew taller and began to throw spray against the windows, she told him stories: of the schooner that found its way home through a fog so thick the crew could not see their own hands, of the night a seal climbed onto the jetty and slept beside the door, and of Tomas's mother, who had painted the tower's red stripe when she was barely his age. By evening the wind howled around the gallery, and Mirela let him strike the match himself. The flame caught, steadied, and grew, and the beam swept out across the dark water, a slow and patient signal telling every sailor still at sea that someone was watching and that the shore was near.

## Hop 20

At the end of Harbor Lane stood a lighthouse that had not guided a ship in forty years. The coast guard had replaced it with an automated beacon on the breakwater, and the old tower had slowly surrendered to salt, wind, and gulls. When Tobias Wren bought it at auction for less than the price of a used car, his friends told him he had lost his mind. Tobias only shrugged and began hauling buckets of rust and broken glass down the spiral staircase. In the keeper's cottage he found a wooden chest wedged beneath the floorboards. Inside were logbooks dating back more than a century, each page recording weather, passing vessels, and the occasional rescue. The keepers had also written about their lives: a daughter's first steps, a winter when the bay froze solid, a storm that tore away the boathouse. Tobias read them by lantern light, night after night, until the voices of those long-gone keepers felt like old friends. He began sharing the entries online, one each evening. Soon letters arrived from strangers whose ancestors had sailed past the tower, served as keepers, or been pulled from the surf by lamplight. A retired fisherman mailed a photograph of his grandfather standing on the gallery rail. A schoolteacher brought her students to hear the stories aloud. By summer, volunteers had repainted the tower white and red. On the anniversary of its final voyage, Tobias climbed to the top and switched on a small lamp. It guided no ships, he admitted, but it helped people find their way home.

## Hop 30

In the hill town of Vellmoor, the great clock above the market square had been frozen at a quarter past four for as long as anyone could remember. Merchants arranged their days by church bells and the angle of the sun. The town elders claimed its gears were rusted beyond repair. The grandmothers whispered that time itself had stopped there on the day of the great flood. A curious boy named Tobin, who swept the bakery floor each morning, noticed that the minute hand trembled whenever the wind blew from the north. One gray afternoon, he slipped through a loose panel at the base of the tower and climbed the creaking ladder inside. At the top, he discovered a cramped chamber filled with brass wheels, coiled springs, and dusty sketches pinned to the walls. A frail woman named Isolde sat at a workbench, filing a tiny cog by candlelight. She had been the clockmaker's apprentice, she confessed, and had stopped the clock herself when her master drowned in the flood, unable to bear the sound of hours passing without him. Yet she had never stopped tending it, rebuilding the mechanism piece by piece in secret. Tobin begged her to show him how it worked. All through the autumn, he learned to oil pivots, balance pendulums, and read her careful drawings. On the morning of the harvest festival, the whole town looked up as the bells rang out and the hands began to move. Isolde smiled through her tears, because at last time was moving forward, and so was she.

## Hop 40

In the hill town of Brevik, the old clock tower had stopped at seventeen minutes past four on the night of a great frost, and no one had wound it since. Moss crept over its face, pigeons nested behind the numerals, and the townspeople had long ago stopped glancing up to check the hour. Market days began late, the baker's bread burned in forgotten ovens, and the school bell rang whenever the headmaster happened to remember. A boy named Emil, who swept the cobbled square each morning, often stared up at the frozen hands and wondered what they would look like moving again. One foggy dawn he heard a soft ticking drifting down from the tower, uneven and hesitant, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm. He found the narrow door unlocked and climbed the creaking wooden ladder into the gloom. Inside, among gears as large as cartwheels, a woman named Marit was oiling a rusted spring with a tiny brass can. She had been the town's clockmaker before her eyesight faded, she explained, and she had been coming each week to coax the mechanism back to life by touch alone. But she could no longer see which teeth were cracked. Emil began visiting every afternoon. Marit taught him to file new cogs, balance the pendulum, and listen for the faintest grinding that meant trouble. By midsummer the gears turned smoothly. At noon on the longest day, Emil pulled the lever. The great hands lurched forward, the bell rang twelve clear notes across Brevik, and everyone in the square looked up and smiled.

## Hop 50

In the hill town of Varneth, the great clock in the square had stopped at a quarter past nine on the morning of the spring flood. For years afterward, its frozen hands looked down on the market stalls, and people began to forget what hour it truly was. Bakers opened late, the schoolbell rang at odd times, and the evening coach often left half its passengers standing in the road. A boy named Emrys, who swept the steps of the town hall for a few coins, liked to sit beneath the clock tower during his lunch and listen to the silence where its ticking used to be. One drizzly afternoon he heard a faint tapping from inside the tower door. He pushed it open and found an elderly woman kneeling among rusted gears, sorting springs into little piles by lamplight. Her name was Maren, and she had cast the clock's bronze bell when she was barely older than he was. She remembered every cog and pendulum weight, but her hands now trembled too much to set a pin or tighten a screw. Emrys offered to be her hands. Each day after sweeping, he climbed the narrow ladder to oil the wheels, file away the rust, and replace the broken teeth she carefully sketched for him. Maren told him stories of the old guilds and taught him how to hear when a gear ran true. On the first morning of summer, they gave the pendulum a gentle push together. The gears caught, the hands began to move, and the bell rang out across Varneth, calling the whole town back to time.

## Hop 56

By September the wildflowers had gone to seed, and the wind off the water carried the first sharp promise of winter. A letter arrived from the shipping company, sealed with red wax, and Wren read it twice on the bench before she dared to show Petter. The company was offering her a proper wage, a new oilskin coat, and the title of head keeper beginning the first of the year. Petter read it slowly, his lips moving, then folded it along its creases and handed it back without a word. That evening he did not come up for the lighting. Wren found him in the storeroom, sorting through a trunk of old things: a cracked compass, a bundle of letters tied with twine, and a faded photograph of a young man standing where she stood now, squinting into a wind that had blown forty years before. "I was nineteen," he said, not looking up. "Old Maren kept this light before me. She taught me to trim a wick so it never smoked." He pressed the compass into Wren's palm. Its needle trembled, then settled north. "You won't need it," he said. "But a keeper should carry something from the one who came before." Wren climbed the stairs alone that night. She lit the lamp, wound the clockwork, and watched the beam swing out across the black water, steady and patient, the way it always had. Somewhere past the Needle Rocks a fishing boat altered its course, and Wren, holding the compass tight, wrote the time in the logbook.

## Hop 57

Winter came early that year, rolling in on a gale that stripped the last leaves from the hawthorn and piled grey foam against the landing. Petter moved more slowly now, and some mornings Wren found him asleep in the chair by the stove, a blanket slipping from his knees and the kettle long gone cold. She took over the morning chores without being asked: splitting driftwood, scraping salt from the lantern glass, checking the oil stores against the ledger. In the afternoons she read to him from the bundle of letters, Maren's cramped handwriting describing storms and shipwrecks and a winter so cold the gulls froze to the rail. Petter listened with his eyes closed, sometimes correcting a date or a name, sometimes laughing softly at a memory she could not share. On the shortest night of the year, the fog rolled in so thick that Wren could not see the Needle Rocks from the gallery. She sounded the horn every two minutes until her arms ached, and near midnight she heard footsteps on the stairs. Petter appeared in the doorway, wrapped in his old coat, breathing hard from the climb. Without a word he took the handle and worked it beside her, matching her rhythm. They kept the horn going until dawn thinned the fog to silver, and a trawler slid past safely, its crew waving from the deck. Afterward they sat together on the top step, sharing a cup of tea gone lukewarm. "Maren would have liked you," Petter said. Wren smiled and wrote the time in the logbook.

## Hop 58

By midwinter the supply boat stopped coming altogether, and Wren learned to ration everything: oil, flour, the precious tin of tea leaves Petter kept hidden behind the almanacs. The days shrank to a few grey hours, and the lamp burned longer than it rested. Petter no longer climbed the tower, but each evening he sat at the foot of the stairs and listened as she wound the clockwork, calling out corrections when the gears ran rough. One afternoon, searching for spare wicks, Wren found a small oilskin packet wedged beneath a loose board in the storeroom. Inside was a final letter from Maren, unopened, its seal still bright red. She carried it to Petter with trembling hands. He turned it over for a long time before giving it back. "You read it," he said. "I never could." The letter was short. Maren wrote of the fever that had taken hold of her, of her fear that the light would go dark with no one to tend it. She wrote that she hoped someone would come, someone patient and stubborn, who loved the sea without trusting it. "Keep the flame for me," the last line read. Wren folded the page carefully and laid it on the table between them. Petter's eyes were wet, but he was smiling. That night a hard frost settled over the island, and the stars came out sharp and countless above the black water. Wren trimmed the wick, polished the lens until it shone, and watched the beam sweep out across the waves, steady and unbroken, all the way to morning.

## Hop 59

Spring came slowly, first as a softening in the wind and then as a thin green haze across the rocks below the tower. The ice loosened its grip on the cove, and one bright morning Wren spotted a sail on the horizon, small and white against the pale sky. She ran down the stairs two at a time, shouting for Petter, who shuffled to the doorway wrapped in his heavy wool coat. The supply boat had returned, and with it came a young inspector from the mainland, clutching a leather satchel and a list of questions. He had heard the light kept burning all winter, he said, and he had come to learn who was responsible. Petter pointed at Wren without a word. The inspector climbed the tower with her, examining the clockwork, the lens, and the logbook she had filled in her careful, cramped hand. He asked how she had managed the oil, how she had repaired the cracked pane in February, and how she had kept awake through the longest nights. She answered each question plainly. When they came down again, he wrote for a long time before handing Petter a folded paper stamped with the lighthouse board's seal. It named Wren as assistant keeper, with wages and a promise of supplies. Petter read it twice, then pressed it into her hands. That evening they opened the last of the tea leaves to celebrate. Wren tucked the appointment inside Maren's letter and placed them both on the shelf beside the almanacs, where the lamplight could find them.

## Hop 60

Summer arrived with long, golden evenings that barely darkened before dawn. Wren took to her new duties with the same stubborn care she had shown all winter, rising before Petter to trim the wicks and polish the brass until it caught the sun. The supply boat came every fortnight now, bringing oil, flour, and sometimes a bundle of newspapers that she read aloud to Petter on the bench outside the door. His cough had eased with the warmer weather, though he still moved slowly on the stairs, and more than once she found him resting halfway up, one hand pressed flat against the cool stone. In July a storm swept in from the west without warning. The sky turned the color of slate, and the waves climbed the rocks until spray lashed the lantern room windows. Wren kept the light turning through the night while Petter sat below, feeding the stove and listening to the wind. Near midnight she saw a fishing boat struggling toward the cove, its mast bent low and its lantern swinging wildly. She rang the fog bell again and again, guiding the boat past the reef until it slid into calmer water. At dawn, three soaked fishermen knocked at the door, shaking and grateful. Petter wrapped them in blankets while Wren boiled water for tea. One of them, an old man with a gray beard, studied her face for a long moment. He said he had known a keeper named Maren once, years ago, and that Wren had her eyes. Wren said nothing, but her hands trembled as she poured.
