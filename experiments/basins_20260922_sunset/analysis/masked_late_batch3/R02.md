## Hop 10

The piano tuner came on a Monday, which Ines had already learned was the day the shop belonged to no one in particular. He did not knock. He stood on the step with his case in both hands, as if he had been sent for and was waiting to be told he had the wrong address.

"There isn't a piano," Ines said.

"There was," he said. "Upstairs. She sold it in the winter. I'm not here for the piano."

He wanted the bench. She had not known there was a bench, but he described it so exactly—the hinge that caught, the lid that had to be lifted from the left—that she went up and found it under a dust sheet in the room with the sloped ceiling. Inside, instead of sheet music, there were letters, tied in bundles by year. The oldest was from before Ines was born.

She carried the bench down herself. He did not offer to help, and she understood that this was a kind of courtesy.

"You'll want to read those," he said, when she set it before him.

"Are they mine to read?"

He considered the question longer than she expected. "They're the shop's," he said. "Which is the same thing now, or will be."

He left the letters. He took only the bench, tilting it through the doorway with the ease of someone who had done it before. The bell rang once behind him, then settled.

Ins sat on the floor where the bench had been and untied the first bundle, and the paper made the sound of something being let out.

## Hop 20

The door opened inward on a breath of air that was not stale. That was the first wrong thing. Eleven years of a locked room should have smelled of dust and mouse and forgotten damp, and instead it smelled faintly of coffee, of soap, of a window recently shut.

The room was small and square and lived in. A cot along the far wall, blanket folded with a neatness that felt like an apology. A table under the sloped ceiling with a candle stub, a saucer of pins, a stack of paper cut to the same size as the letters. A chair pushed back at an angle, as though someone had stood up quickly and not sat down again.

And on the floorboards, running in a thin bright line from the table to the far wall, a strip of light. She followed it with her eyes to a knot in the wood where the light came through, and beneath it, worn pale by years of a body lying pressed close, a shallow hollow the shape of a shoulder and a cheek.

She knelt. She put her eye to the knothole.

Below her was the back room of the shop, her lamp still burning on the shelf where she left it every night, the counter, the door she locked, the stool where she sat to mend the account books. Everything she did alone. Everything she had believed no one saw.

Behind her, on the stairs, a board took a weight and held its breath.

Ines did not turn around. She said, quietly, to the floor, "You can come in. It's your room."

## Hop 30

The key was on the nail behind the stove, where it had always been, though the nail had bent under the weight of years and the key hung crooked. She took it down and felt the cold of it in her palm. Tomas watched from the threshold with his hat still on, as if he had not yet decided whether he was staying.

The door to the back room had swollen in its frame. She put her shoulder to it and it gave with a sound like a held breath let out. Inside, the shutters were closed, and the air smelled of lamp oil and the dry sweetness of paper.

Her mother's loom stood against the far wall, threaded. A length of cloth hung from it, half finished, the pattern stopping mid-row where a hand had simply lifted away and not returned. Blue, and a gray that might once have been green.

"He wouldn't let anyone touch it," Tomas said behind her. "Not even to dust."

"Did he sit with it?"

"Evenings. Winter, mostly."

She crossed the room and put her fingers to the warp threads. They were slack but whole. Somewhere a mouse had tried and given up. She pressed a thread and it answered with a low note, almost a hum, and she remembered that sound from a room she had not stood in for twenty years.

Tomas shifted in the doorway. "I can take it apart. If you'd rather."

She found the shuttle where it had been left, in the trough of the beater, its bobbin still wound. She turned it over once. Then she sat down on the bench, which held, and drew the shuttle through.

## Hop 40

The stairs going down were not the stairs he had climbed. He was sure of it, though he could not have said how, only that these were narrower and turned the other way, and the light on them came from below rather than above, a grey daylight leaking up through the treads as if the ground floor had been left open to the sky.

He counted the landings. There were more than there should have been. At the fourth he passed a door with his name on it, in letters he recognised from the drawer, and did not stop. At the sixth he passed a window, and through it he saw the street from an angle that was impossible, the crossing laid out beneath him like a diagram, the boy a small dark mark at its edge, still turned toward the building, still looking up.

Tomas began to take the steps two at a time.

Somewhere above him a door closed, softly, the way a door closes on a room that has been put in order. He did not look back. He thought about the pen, which he had left on the desk, and about the warmth of it, and about whose thumb had worn that groove, and he found that he did not want the answer as much as he had a few minutes ago.

The last flight ended not in a hall but in a doorway, open, and beyond it the pavement, ordinary and wet. He stepped through. The boy had not moved. He was still waiting for his hand to be taken.

Tomas crossed toward him, and the lights, obligingly, changed.

## Hop 50

Marisol ran the ferry the way her father had run it, which is to say she ran it late and without apology. The schedule nailed to the dock post had faded to a suggestion. Passengers came expecting a timetable and found instead a woman who crossed the water when the water agreed to be crossed.

"You said nine," a man told her, stepping aboard with a suitcase that had clearly been packed in anger. Leather corners, brass clasp, a luggage tag with no name.

"I said around nine."

"It's half past."

Marisol cast off the line and let the engine find its voice. She did not look at the suitcase. She had learned that people wanted her to ask about the suitcase, and that it was almost never where the trouble lived.

"Going or coming back?"

He frowned, the way they always did when the question was not about the ferry. "Going."

"How long since you decided?"

The gulls did their slow, uneven circling above them. Somewhere a bell buoy considered the swell and rang anyway.

"Last night," he said. "Around three."

"Then you're not late." She eased the bow toward the far shore. "You're early. You've been running on a clock that started at three in the morning. The rest of us are still on yesterday's."

He stared at the wake for a long moment, and then he laughed, once, surprised, and set the suitcase down flat instead of upright.

"What's the fare?"

"Same as coming back," said Marisol. "In case you need it."

## Hop 56

Marit ran the ferry the way her father had run it, which is to say she made the crossing at seven whether or not anyone stood on the dock, and she never explained why she sounded the horn three times leaving and once returning. The habit predated the boat itself. She had cast off, by her own loose count, some eleven thousand mornings, and she had quit counting because the number had started to sound less like a promise and more like a debt.

A man came down the ramp on the last ice-free morning of the year with a coat too thin for the wind and a suitcase he never set down. He stopped at the edge of the pier instead of stepping aboard, which told her most of what she needed to know.

"Is this the boat to the island?"

"It's the only boat."

"I meant—does anyone ever miss it and just stay?"

Marit checked the lines and started the engine. She had learned that people asked about staying when they were already half gone, and asked whether it was the only boat when they wanted someone else to decide.

"Same water," she said. "Different tide."

He laughed without weight in it and shifted the suitcase to his other hand. Out past the breakwater the swell came in from the north and the deck began its slow roll, the way it always did. He gripped the rail.

"Does it always do that?"

"Every crossing. Same stretch."

"Then you'd think you'd stop bracing."

"You'd think." She eased the throttle. "But I want to brace. The morning the swell feels gentle is the morning I stop watching the bow."

Ahead, the island's single dock light steadied and held. He didn't move toward the cabin.

"We tie up at eight," she said. "You don't have to decide yet."

"When do I have to?"

"At eight," said Marit. "That's what the horn is for."

## Hop 57

Teodor kept the lighthouse the way his mother had kept it, which is to say he climbed the hundred and twelve steps at dusk whether or not the automatic lamp had already come on, and he never explained why he wiped the lens with his left hand only. The habit predated the electricity. He had made the climb, by his own rough count, some nine thousand evenings, and he had stopped counting because the number had begun to sound less like a vocation and more like a sentence.

A woman came up the cliff path on the first evening of the fog season with boots too clean for the rocks and a letter she kept folding and unfolding. She stopped at the door instead of knocking, which told him most of what he needed to know.

"Is this the light for the strait?"

"It's the only light."

"I meant—does anyone ever stop seeing it and just keep going?"

Teodor checked the oil gauge out of old habit and started up the stairs. He had learned that people asked about being lost when they were already half found, and asked whether it was the only light when they wanted a reason not to look for another.

"Same sea," he said. "Different dark."

She followed him up, the letter in her fist. Near the top the wind found the gaps in the stone and the tower gave its faint, familiar shiver. She flattened a palm against the wall.

"Does it always do that?"

"Every gale. Same course."

"Then you'd think you'd stop flinching."

"You'd think." He turned the crank. "But I want to flinch. The night the tower feels solid is the night I stop checking the mortar."

Below, a ship's running lights blinked and held their line. She didn't move toward the window.

"The beam turns at nine," he said. "You don't have to read it yet."

"When do I have to?"

"At nine," said Teodor. "That's what the light is for."

## Hop 58

Marisol ran the ferry the way her father had run it, which is to say she left the dock at ten past six whether or not the schedule board said six, and she never explained why she touched the rail twice before casting off. The habit predated the diesel engine. She had made the crossing, by her own rough count, some eleven thousand mornings, and she had stopped counting because the number had begun to sound less like a livelihood and more like a tide table she could not step out of.

A boy came down the ramp on the first morning of the ice season with a duffel too heavy for his shoulders and a photograph he kept turning face down and face up. He stopped at the gangway instead of boarding, which told her most of what she needed to know.

"Is this the boat to the island?"

"It's the only boat."

"I meant—does anyone ever ride it out and just not come back?"

Marisol checked the mooring line out of old habit and started the engine. She had learned that people asked about not returning when they had already half decided, and asked whether it was the only boat when they wanted permission not to look for another.

"Same water," she said. "Different shore."

He came aboard, the photograph in his fist. Midway across the wind found the open channel and the hull gave its low, familiar shudder. He gripped the bench.

"Does it always do that?"

"Every chop. Same crossing."

"Then you'd think you'd stop bracing."

"You'd think." She adjusted the wheel. "But I want to brace. The day the water feels flat is the day I stop watching the horizon."

Ahead, the island's dock lights blinked and held steady. He didn't look toward them.

"We land at seven," she said. "You don't have to step off yet."

"When do I have to?"

"At seven," said Marisol. "That's what the boat is for."

## Hop 59

Teodor kept the lighthouse the way his mother had kept it, which is to say he lit the lamp at twenty to dusk whether or not the almanac said dusk, and he never explained why he counted the stairs aloud on the way up but not on the way down. The habit predated the electric bulb. He had climbed the tower, by his own careless tally, some nine thousand evenings, and he had stopped tallying because the number had begun to sound less like a duty and more like a sentence he had handed himself.

A woman came up the shore path on the first evening of the fog season with a suitcase too small for a long stay and a letter she kept folding and unfolding. She stopped at the base of the tower instead of knocking, which told him most of what he needed to know.

"Is this the light for the strait?"

"It's the only light."

"I meant—does anyone out there ever see it and just keep going?"

Teodor checked the oil gauge out of old habit and struck the match. He had learned that people asked about ships passing when they were already half aboard one, and asked whether it was the only light when they wanted permission not to search for another.

"Same beam," he said. "Different eyes."

She came inside, the letter in her fist. Halfway up, the wind found the gallery door and the iron stairs rang their thin, familiar note. She caught the rail.

"Does it always do that?"

"Every gust. Same tower."

"Then you'd think you'd stop flinching."

"You'd think." He trimmed the wick. "But I want to flinch. The night the stairs go quiet is the night I stop listening for the sea."

Below, a mast light blinked and steadied. She didn't look toward it.

"The lamp turns at dusk," he said. "You don't have to decide yet."

"When do I have to?"

"At dusk," said Teodor. "That's what the light is for."

## Hop 60

Marit ran the ferry the way her father had run it, which is to say she cast off at ten past the hour whether or not the timetable agreed, and she never explained why she touched the bell twice before leaving the dock but only once on return. The habit predated the diesel engine. She had made the crossing, by her own loose reckoning, some eleven thousand times, and she had stopped reckoning because the number had begun to sound less like a livelihood and more like a debt she was paying to herself.

A man came down the quay on the first morning of the ice season with a rucksack too full for a day trip and a photograph he kept turning face down. He stood at the edge of the ramp instead of stepping on, which told her most of what she needed to know.

"Is this the boat for the island?"

"It's the only boat."

"I meant—does anyone ever ride it over and not come back?"

Marit checked the fuel line out of old habit and coiled the bow rope. She had learned that people asked about one-way passengers when they were already halfway to becoming one, and asked whether it was the only boat when they wanted someone else to close the other doors.

"Same water," she said. "Different reasons."

He boarded, the photograph in his pocket. Midway across, the wind caught the wheelhouse door and the hull gave its low, familiar shudder. He gripped the rail.

"Does it always do that?"

"Every swell. Same boat."

"Then you'd think you'd stop bracing."

"You'd think." She eased the throttle. "But I want to brace. The crossing I stop feeling is the crossing I stop respecting."

Ahead, the island's harbor light steadied. He didn't look toward it.

"We tie up at half past," she said. "You don't have to decide yet."

"When do I have to?"

"At half past," said Marit. "That's what the dock is for."
