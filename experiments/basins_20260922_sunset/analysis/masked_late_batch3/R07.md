## Hop 10

The ferry ran twice a day in winter, and Tomas had driven it for twenty-two years before the council replaced the old diesel with a new electric hull that steered itself between the pilings. They kept him on for one more season to sit in the wheelhouse and watch the screens. He did not touch anything. He was there, the letter said, for the reassurance of passengers.

On the first crossing of the morning there were rarely passengers to reassure. A woman with a bicycle, sometimes. A boy going to the mainland school, asleep against the window. Tomas stood with his hands behind his back and looked out at the channel, where the tide ran hard between the island and the shore, and where he had learned the eddies by feel long before anyone drew them on a chart.

The new boat crossed in eleven minutes. His old one had taken fourteen, sometimes eighteen when the wind came from the north and the current set wrong. He had thought, in those years, that the extra minutes were a kind of failure. Now he found he missed them.

Halfway across, the boy woke and came to the wheelhouse door. He asked what the screens meant. Tomas told him about depth, about speed over ground, about the small green triangle that was themselves. The boy nodded and watched the shore approach.

"Do you still steer it?" he asked.

"No," Tomas said. Then, after a moment: "I watch the water."

The boy seemed to find this a reasonable answer, and went back to his seat.

## Hop 20

The lighthouse at Vesterholm was automated the spring Anders turned seventy-one, and the engineers left on the same morning his brother's letter stopped arriving. He stood at the foot of the tower watching their van take the coast road, the last envelope from March still folded in his coat pocket, and for a long while the two silences kept company in him: the lamp that would now turn without a hand on it and the mailbox at the gate with nothing inside.

He climbed the stairs anyway. Forty years had taught his legs the count, and the lantern room did not object to an old man arriving at dusk with nothing to do. The brass still wanted polishing, the logbook still lay open on its shelf, and when the beam swung out over the skerries he found himself noting the time in the margin as if someone downstream would read it.

The ships passed farther out now, guided by satellites, and did not need him. He knew that. He still stood at the glass when a trawler came in past the reef, lifting his hand the way he always had, and once or twice a light blinked back, which might have been habit on their side too.

What unsettled him was how little the machinery changed the work. He had thought himself the keeper, the one the light depended on. Now he understood he had mostly been its companion. The lamp had never needed him to burn; it had needed someone to see the dark it was pushing against and to say, quietly, that the coast was still there.

He kept saying it.

## Hop 30

The mill at Hollins Ford stopped turning the spring Agnes's mother was buried, and the same week the co-operative wrote to say flour would come by lorry now from the roller mill in town, bagged and stamped and cheaper by the hundredweight. Agnes read the letter at the kitchen table, then went out and oiled the gears as she had every Saturday she could remember.

She still opened the sluice at dawn. There was no grain to grind, but her hands knew the iron wheel better than they knew a pen, and the water did not seem to have heard that the wheel had been let go. She would stand in the meal loft as the light came up through the dust, and down the lane the lorry would pass with its engine coughing, and she would count the sacks on the flatbed the way her mother had taught her to count the turns.

The bakers said the town flour was whiter and never went weevily. She believed them. She still watched the carts come up from the farms of a morning, naming each by the sound of its axle, and once old Pruett left a sack of barley on the step without knocking, which was either loyalty or forgetfulness, and Agnes did not ask.

What surprised her was how the river kept coming without needing her. She had thought herself the mill. Now she understood she had mostly been the one who let it through. The water had never required her hand on the sluice; it had required someone in the loft to believe each morning that the turning mattered.

She opened the gate again the next dawn.

## Hop 40

The lighthouse at Carrow Point had been automated in 1987, and for the eleven years after that Maura kept the garden anyway. Nobody paid her. The commissioners had offered the keeper's cottage at a rent that was more gesture than sum, and she took it because she had been born in it and because the alternative was a bungalow in town with a view of the chemist's.

The light came on by itself at dusk now, a soft click somewhere in the tower that she could hear from the kitchen if the wind was down. She had stopped flinching at it around the third year. Before that, every evening, some part of her had risen to go up the stairs and found there was nothing to do when she got there.

She grew potatoes and kale and a row of sweet pea against the south wall, which was the only wall that gave them a chance. Fishermen coming in past the point could see the colour from the water and said so, the few times they met her in the shop. She liked that they saw it. She did not say so.

In the spring of the twelfth year a man from the commissioners drove out to tell her the cottage was to be sold. He was young and apologetic and kept looking at the sweet pea instead of at her. She gave him tea. When he had gone she went up the tower, for the first time in years, and stood by the lamp until it clicked on beside her.

## Hop 50

He closed at six, as he always did, though there had been nothing to close against. He turned the sign and stood a moment with his hand on the bolt, listening to the street settle. A bicycle. Someone calling a dog in. The ordinary sounds, and under them the strip of tar giving off the last of its heat, or so he imagined, since a man cannot hear tar cooling and he knew it.

He did not go up to the flat. He went instead to the back room and sat with the ledger open on his knees and turned the pages without reading them. His father's hand for the first forty pages, then his own, the two scripts so alike that he had once lost an afternoon trying to find the exact line where one stopped and the other began. He found it now without looking. March, the year of the flood. Nails, a pound. The same figure.

That was what troubled him, if he was honest, and he tried at this hour to be honest. Not that she knew the price. Anyone might know it who had once bought nails. It was that she had written it on a card and left the card where he would find it, as a man leaves a coin on a step to see whether the boy who sweeps is the kind who pockets it or the kind who hands it in.

He did not know which kind he was. He had never had cause to find out. He closed the ledger and sat in the dark, and outside, very faintly, something on the road set and went hard.

## Hop 56

The ferry left at four, and she had known this for a month, and she had told no one. The timetable was pinned inside the wardrobe door where he would not look, behind the dresses she no longer wore, and she had checked it every evening as though the hour might change on her.

Her mother's suitcase was under the bed. It was the brown one with the brass corners, the one that had come back from Boston in 1958 with nothing in it but a missal and a photograph, and she had packed it on Sunday while he was at the match. Two blouses, the grey skirt, the shoes with the strap. She had packed it and then unpacked it and then packed it again, and each time the folding was the same and the deciding was not.

He was in the yard now. She could hear the gate, the dog, the bucket set down on the flags. He would come in at half three and want his dinner and eat it without speaking, and she would sit across from him and watch the clock over his shoulder, and at ten to four he would go out again to see to the calves. That was when a person would leave, if they were leaving.

The passport was in her handbag. It had been in her handbag for a fortnight.

She pulled the suitcase out and set it on the bed and looked at it. Then she opened it, and took out the blouses one by one, and hung them back where they had been, and went down to put the potatoes on.

## Hop 57

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the day the post was always late, and she had it read and folded and back in its envelope before the van had turned at the end of the lane. It was from her sister in Liverpool. It said what the last three had said, only shorter.

She put it in the drawer with the others, under the tea towels, and went out to feed the hens.

The hens did not care about Liverpool. They came at her in their fussing way, all of them at once, and she scattered the meal in a wide arc so they would spread out and she could stand a minute in the middle of them without anyone needing anything from her. The morning was cold and clear. Across the field the ash trees stood in a line the way they had stood since before she was born, and she thought, as she often did, that a tree had the right idea. You put down roots and you stayed put and nobody wrote to ask why.

He was in the shed with the engine of the tractor in pieces on a sack. She could hear him not swearing, which was worse than if he did.

At eleven she made the tea and brought his out to him and set it on the upturned crate, and he nodded without looking up. She stood a moment watching his hands.

"Bridie wrote," she said.

"Did she."

That was the whole of it. She went back in and took the letter from the drawer and read it once more, and then she put it on the range and watched the corner catch.

## Hop 58

The bus left from outside the chemist's at ten past eight, and he was there by half seven with his good coat on and nothing in his hands. He had thought about a bag and decided against it. A bag was a statement. A man standing at a bus stop with no bag could be going anywhere, or nowhere, and that was how he wanted it.

The chemist's girl came to open up and gave him a look. He nodded at her the way you nod at weather.

His daughter had rung on Sunday, the way she rang every Sunday, and had said the thing she said every Sunday, which was that the room was there and the door was open and he needn't decide anything. He had said he'd think on it. He had been thinking on it since March.

The town was waking up around him. The butcher's van. The two lads from the garage with their cans of something. He knew every one of them and not one of them knew he was leaving, if he was leaving, which he had not yet decided.

The bus came round the corner. He watched it slow. The driver was young, a stranger, and looked at him with the blank patience of a man who did not know this was anything.

He put his hand in his pocket and found the coins were there. He had counted them out the night before, exact.

The door hissed open.

He stood a moment longer. Then he got on.

## Hop 59

The ferry ran twice a day in winter, and she had missed the morning one on purpose. She told herself it was the tide, or the packing, but the truth was she had sat at the kitchen table with her coat on and watched the clock go past nine and felt nothing move in her.

The cottage was already someone else's. The papers had gone through in November. She had a fortnight's grace and had used eleven days of it walking the same paths, touching the same gates, as if the place might remember her better for it.

Her son had been patient. He was always patient, in the way of people who have already won. The flat in the city had a lift and a warden and a view of a car park, and he had said the word convenient so many times it had stopped sounding like English.

She locked the door and put the key under the stone where it had always been, though there was no one now who would look for it there.

The harbour was quiet. A man she half knew was mending a net and did not look up, and she was grateful for that. The boat sat low against the wall, its engine ticking over, a thin line of smoke going straight up in the still air.

She had one bag. She had argued with herself about the second and lost, or won, she was no longer sure which.

The deckhand held out his hand. She looked at it a while, the rope burns across the palm, the salt in the creases.

Then she took it, and stepped across.

## Hop 60

The library closed at six on Thursdays, and he had timed it so he would be the last one there. Not for any reason he could have said aloud. He simply wanted to hear the lights go off one bank at a time, the way they always did, and to be the one the caretaker nodded at on the way out.

Forty-one years. He had counted them that morning while shaving and then wished he hadn't. The number sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The new woman was good. He had trained her himself, over the autumn, and she had listened in a way that made him feel both useful and already gone. She knew where the local history was kept and which of the regulars needed the large print and which only pretended to. She would be fine. That was the trouble with it.

He walked the stacks once more. Fiction, then the reference shelves nobody used now, then the children's corner with its low chairs and its faint smell of crayon. He straightened a spine that did not need straightening.

His daughter had bought him a garden bench as a present. It was still in its box in the hall. He had looked at the picture on the side, a man and a woman sitting very upright in a good deal of sunlight, and had not been able to imagine himself into it.

The caretaker was at the door, keys in hand.

He turned off the last lamp himself, and went.
