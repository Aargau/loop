## Hop 10

They returned at the next low tide with two coils of rope and a boat hook. Mara tied one end to the iron staple on the church steps. Daniel carried the other to the skiff, paying it out as Mr. Venn rowed toward the seventh mark.

The rope passed directly over the pale stones.

“Stop there,” Mrs. Bell said.

She lowered the boat hook beside the wall. Its tip struck stone, then slipped into a gap. When she pulled it free, something caught on the hook: a narrow loop of blue cloth.

Mara held it beside the strip they had found the day before. The torn edges fitted together.

“Someone tied this here,” Daniel said.

“And it tore when they pulled it loose,” Mrs. Bell replied. She took the hook again and felt around the gap. This time it struck metal.

Mr. Venn steadied the skiff while Daniel leaned over and reached down. He came up with a ring attached to a flat iron plate. Sand streamed from its edges, but the plate did not move when he tugged it.

From the church steps, Mara called out the distance marked on the rope. Mr. Venn checked the notebook and nodded. It was the distance their father had written beside the seventh mark.

Three taps came through the hull. Everyone went still.

Daniel tapped the plate once with the hook. After a pause, two knocks answered from below.

Mrs. Bell looked toward the channel. The tide had turned. “We leave it for now.”

Mara marked the ring’s position on her drawing. Beneath it, she wrote the time of the answering knocks.

## Hop 20

Their father held the trapdoor open. “Who’s down there?”

A lantern appeared at the foot of the steps, throwing a long shadow across the wall. The person carrying it was a girl in a fisherman’s coat, no older than Daniel. Water swirled around her boots.

“Please,” she said. “Shut it before they see the light.”

The harbor master glanced toward the quay. “Who?”

The girl did not answer. She climbed three steps and held up a small brass box. Its lid bore the same mark as the X on the drawing.

“I found this last night,” she said. “There’s another passage below, but the tide cut me off. I came back at dawn.”

Mara recognized the lantern then: the one she had watched moving along the north wall. “You were looking for the door.”

“I knew it was here somewhere.” The girl looked past Mara at their father. “Is that your ledger?”

He drew it closer to his chest. From inside came a sudden click, as if the box had answered something hidden among its pages.

The harbor master heard it too. His face changed. “Open the ledger.”

Their father took out the folded drawing. A thin brass plate, caught between two pages, slipped free and landed on the paving. It had a round hole at one end.

The girl climbed another step and fitted the plate into a slot beneath the box’s lid. Inside was a key and a scrap of paper, damp but still legible.

Mara read the first line aloud: “Wait until the tide turns.”

## Hop 30

By noon, the mud had begun to dry in plates. Mara watched her father kneel beside the grate and lift away a clot of weed. Beneath it lay a narrow brass key.

The woman did not reach for it. “Elias carried that,” she said. “He said the gate had two locks.”

“There’s only one,” Mara’s father said.

“Now there is.”

He turned the key over. Salt had eaten most of its teeth, but one side bore the same eight strokes cut into the bell. Mara counted them twice.

From outside came the harbor master’s voice. He had found footprints in the mud beyond the gate: a single trail leading toward shore. Mara hurried down after him. The prints were small and close together, as if whoever made them had been carrying something heavy. They ended beneath the lantern window.

There, pressed deep into the mud, was the round impression of the blue float.

Mara looked up. The float still hung by the door upstairs. Its paint had left a faint mark on the woman’s palm.

Her father joined her at the window. For a moment neither of them spoke. Then a soft clang sounded overhead, no louder than a spoon against a cup.

They climbed the stairs. The woman stood by the lantern with the bell in her hand. She had rung it once, and was waiting.

Across the emptied harbor, where the old channel curved behind the breakwater, another bell answered. Mara could see no one in the mud. Then the distant light appeared, pale even in daylight, and moved slowly toward the gate.

## Hop 40

Mara read her name once, then covered it with her thumb. On the other side of the gate, something scraped softly against the stones.

“Is she still there?” the woman asked.

Mara looked through the tear. The eye was gone. Farther back, where the passage met the water, a child stood with her back to them. She held the shell against her ear.

The harbor master eased away from the pole. It stayed upright. “We should go before the tide turns.”

The man from the boat did not move. He was staring at the photograph.

“You knew,” Mara said.

“I knew there were two of you.” His voice was hoarse. “After the gate closed, your mother found you on the quay. We thought that was the end of it.”

Mara studied the children in the picture. Their faces blurred together except for the girl standing apart. She remembered the cold stone beneath her shoes, the bell ringing overhead, and someone calling her name from the wrong side of the wall.

The child in the passage turned. Mara could not see her face, only the white shell held tight in one fist. Then she walked into the dark.

The woman touched Mara’s sleeve. “Come home.”

Mara folded the photograph and put it in her pocket. She waited until the last ripple disappeared from the water before following the others up the steps. Halfway to the street, she stopped. For the first time in years, her pocket was empty, and she let it stay that way.

## Hop 50

By morning, the child had learned where they kept the spoons. She laid four beside the breakfast bowls, then paused and added a fifth.

“Who’s that for?” Mara asked.

The child glanced toward the cupboard. “He might be hungry.”

Their mother put the fifth spoon back in the drawer. No one objected.

Rain tapped at the window. Mara’s sister sat with her elbows on the table, watching the child stir honey into her porridge. In the night, she had told them what she could remember: the harbor wall, the cold beneath it, a room with no windows where the harbor master counted tides aloud. She could not say how long she had been there. The child had not been able to say either.

After breakfast, Mara took the shell from the cupboard and set it on the windowsill. The child came to stand beside her.

“Do you want to go back?” Mara asked.

The child shook her head, but she kept her eyes on the shell. “I left someone there.”

Mara’s sister looked up. For a moment, she seemed about to speak. Instead, she crossed the kitchen and took the child’s hand.

Their mother opened the drawer again. She found the fifth spoon and placed it beside the shell, its handle pointing toward the cupboard.

They went about their morning. Mara washed the bowls. Her sister helped the child fasten a button on her coat. When the rain stopped, a thin line of water appeared beneath the cupboard door. It ran across the dry floor and came to rest at Mara’s feet.

## Hop 56

Mara waited until the water fell below the crossbeam. The pale thing was a strip of canvas, caught on a nail. She let out a breath, but her sister was already climbing down the ladder.

“Come back,” their mother called.

“I can see the ledge.” Her sister’s voice rose from beneath the pier. “There’s a door.”

Mara passed the chart to their mother and followed. Barnacles scraped her palms as she lowered herself onto the narrow shelf of stone. In the shadows, her sister stood before a wooden hatch set into the seawall. Its brass lock was furred with salt.

The child’s key turned easily. Beyond the hatch lay a chamber that smelled of mud and old rope. Daylight entered through cracks overhead. Against one wall rested a small boat, its bow marked with their father’s initials.

Their mother called again, nearer this time. Mara lifted the boat’s cover. Beneath it were a coil of dry line, a lantern, and a tin box. Inside the box, wrapped in oilcloth, was a notebook.

The first page bore a date from the morning the boat vanished. In her father’s handwriting were three words: He is alive.

Her sister read them over Mara’s shoulder. “The boy?”

From outside came the child’s voice, asking why everyone had gone under the pier. Mara closed the notebook. Its remaining pages were thick with writing, but the tide had begun to turn. She took her sister’s hand, and together they climbed toward the light.

## Hop 57

Their mother met them at the foot of the ladder, one hand gripping the child’s shoulder. Mara held up the notebook. She did not need to explain what she had found; her mother knew its cover at once.

“Where was it?” she asked.

“With his boat.” Mara glanced back beneath the pier. Water was already washing over the stone ledge.

They carried the notebook to the dunes, where the child could sit out of the wind. Mara opened it to the second page. Their father had written in the hurried, slanting hand he used for weather reports. He had found the boy on an island beyond the channel, injured but alive. A fishing boat had agreed to take them to the mainland.

Then the entries stopped.

Her sister turned the next blank page, and a folded scrap slipped into the sand. It was a receipt from a harbor inn, dated six days after their father’s disappearance. On the back, in smaller writing, was an address.

Their mother took the paper. “I know this street.”

“Is it far?” the child asked.

“Across the bay.” She looked toward the pier, where the hatch had vanished beneath the rising water. “We can catch the afternoon ferry.”

Mara wanted to ask why their father had hidden the boat, and why he had never come home. Instead she wrapped the notebook in her coat. The address gave them somewhere to begin. She helped the child to his feet, and they started along the shore toward town.

## Hop 58

By the time they reached the ferry landing, the child had fallen asleep against their mother’s shoulder. Mara bought four tickets and kept the receipt beside the scrap from the notebook. The address was still legible, though sand had caught in its folds.

Her sister stood at the rail as the ferry pulled away. “Do you think he went there?”

“I think he wanted us to know about it,” Mara said.

Across the bay, the harbor rose in tiers of narrow streets and slate roofs. Their mother had worked there before Mara was born, but she said little during the crossing. Only when they passed the inn named on the receipt did she stop and point uphill.

“That street is near the old hospital.”

The address led them to a blue door between a baker’s shop and a boarded-up tailor’s. No one answered their knock. Mara was about to try again when an upstairs window opened.

A woman looked down at them. Her gaze moved from Mara’s face to the notebook under her arm.

“You’re his daughters,” she said.

Their mother stepped forward. “Is he here?”

The woman gripped the windowsill. For a moment, Mara heard only the ferry’s horn sounding across the water.

“No,” the woman said. “But he was. Come round to the back.”

She closed the window. Mara’s sister took the child’s hand, and together they found the narrow passage beside the bakery. At its end, a gate stood open. Mara held the notebook closer as they went through.

## Hop 59

The passage opened onto a courtyard crowded with empty flowerpots. The woman waited beside a kitchen door, wiping her hands on a towel. Up close, she looked older than Mara had expected.

“He left yesterday,” she said. “Before dawn.”

Mara’s mother stopped so suddenly that her sister nearly walked into her. “Did he say where he was going?”

The woman glanced at the child, now awake and watching them all. “Come inside.”

The kitchen smelled of bread and boiled coffee. On the table lay a folded map, held flat by a chipped blue cup. The woman said her name was Elise. She had known their father at the hospital, years ago, when he delivered supplies there.

“He came to ask about someone,” Elise said. “A patient from that time.”

Their mother pulled out a chair but did not sit. Mara set the notebook on the table. “Was it someone we know?”

Elise touched a place on the map beyond the harbor, where the road ended at a small village. “I told him she was buried here. He went to see for himself.”

For the first time since they had found the notebook, their mother reached for it. She turned past the pages Mara had studied and stopped at a pencil sketch of a young woman standing outside a hospital window.

“I knew her,” their mother said quietly.

Elise nodded. “Then you know why he came.”

But their mother shook her head. She looked at the sketch for a long moment, then turned the notebook so her daughters could see the name written beneath it.

## Hop 60

Beneath the sketch, in their father’s careful handwriting, was a single name: Lena.

Mara’s sister read it aloud. Their mother closed her eyes.

“She was my sister,” she said. “I told you she died before either of you was born.”

Elise drew out the chair their mother had left untouched. This time, she sat. Outside, wind rattled the empty flowerpots across the courtyard.

“She did die,” their mother said. “I went to the funeral.”

“Your father believed it too,” Elise said. “Until last week.”

She lifted the blue cup. Under it lay a photograph, curled at the corners. Mara saw a woman standing in front of a grocery shop, one hand raised against the sun. The date stamped on the back was from three months ago.

“That’s her?” Mara asked.

“I don’t know,” Elise said. “A man brought it to the hospital, looking for records. Your father was there when I opened the letter.”

Their mother held the photograph by its edges. The woman in it had Lena’s dark eyes, but the rest of her face was hard to make out.

“Why didn’t he tell me?” she asked.

Elise looked toward the map. “He wanted to be certain.”

Mara traced the road from the harbor to the village. It ended beside a cemetery, but a second road, faint as a scratch, continued into the hills. Their father had marked it in pencil.

From the courtyard came the sound of an engine. Elise went to the window and pulled back the curtain.

“He rented a car,” she said. “That looks like it now.”
