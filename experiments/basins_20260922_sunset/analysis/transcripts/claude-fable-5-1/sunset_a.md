The sun was setting behind the hills, spilling amber light across the valley in long, slow ribbons. Mara stood at the edge of the orchard with her hands in her coat pockets, watching the shadows of the apple trees stretch toward the farmhouse like fingers reaching for something they could never quite hold. The air had turned cool, carrying the smell of turned earth and woodsmoke from a neighbor's chimney somewhere beyond the ridge.

She had promised herself she would leave before dark. The car was packed, the keys were in her pocket, and the road to the city waited at the bottom of the lane. Yet her feet stayed rooted in the damp grass. Every evening of her childhood had ended like this, with the hills swallowing the sun and her grandmother calling from the porch that supper was on the table. The porch was empty now. The house had been sold that morning, the papers signed in a lawyer's office that smelled of old carpet and coffee.

A crow settled on the fence post beside her and regarded her with one dark, unbothered eye. Mara laughed softly. "You'll have the place to yourself soon," she told it.

The last sliver of gold slipped beneath the ridge. Blue dusk pooled in the hollows. Somewhere a dog barked twice and fell silent. Mara drew a long breath, tasting autumn, and finally turned toward the lane. Behind her, the first star blinked awake over the hills she had loved for thirty years, and would, she suspected, love for the rest of her life.

The tide was coming in when Elias reached the breakwater, the sea folding over itself in gray-green sheets that hissed against the stones. He set down his bucket of paint and looked out at the lighthouse, its white tower gone the color of old bone after another winter of salt and wind. Every spring for forty years he had made this walk, brush in hand, to give the old sentinel a fresh coat before the summer boats began to run.

This year his knees ached more than they used to. The ladder felt taller. Still, he climbed, one rung at a time, the wind tugging at his cap, the gulls wheeling overhead as if to inspect his work. From the gallery he could see the whole harbor: the fishing sheds with their peeling doors, the church steeple, the row of cottages where he had been born and where his daughter now lived with children of her own.

He dipped the brush and began. The paint went on thick and bright, covering the stains of storms he could name by year. The Christmas gale of eighty-one. The nor'easter that took the Marguerite. Each stroke felt like an answer to something the sea had said.

By noon the sun had burned through the haze. Elias sat on the gallery rail, eating bread and cheese from his coat pocket, and watched a pair of seals roll in the shallows. Below him the tower gleamed. Tomorrow the boats would come, and the light would be waiting, as it always had been, white against the water.

The first snow came early that year, before the last of the apples had been picked. Marta stood at the orchard gate with a wooden crate on her hip and watched the flakes settle on the branches, soft and unhurried, as though they had all the time in the world. Behind her the farmhouse chimney sent up a thin gray thread, and she could smell the woodsmoke mixing with the sharp sweetness of fruit left too long on the ground.

Her grandfather had planted these trees the spring he came back from the war. Forty-two of them, in six crooked rows, because he had never owned a measuring line and refused to borrow one. Marta knew each tree by its lean and its scars. The one by the stone wall that split in the ice storm and healed itself into a fork. The runt at the far end that gave the sweetest fruit. The old Baldwin near the well where she had carved her initials at nine and been scolded for it at ten.

She set the crate down and began to pick, working quickly now, her fingers stiff in the cold. The apples came away with a small clean snap. Some were bruised, some already touched by frost, but most were firm and cool and heavy in her palm, and she filled the crate before the snow had done more than dust the grass.

When she turned back toward the house, her footprints were the only marks in the whole white field. She thought of her grandfather walking these rows, and of the children who might someday walk them after her, and she carried the apples inside.

## Hop 4

The lighthouse had been dark for eleven years when Tomas bought it, sight unseen, from a county auction listing that described it as 'structure, coastal, needs work.' He drove out on a Tuesday in March with a thermos of coffee and a set of keys that turned out to fit nothing, and he stood at the base of the tower for a long time before he tried the door. It was unlocked. It had probably always been unlocked.

Inside, the spiral stairs were rusted but sound, and he climbed them slowly, counting. One hundred and six. At the top the lamp room was full of gulls' feathers and the glass was fogged with salt, but when he wiped a circle clear with his sleeve he could see the whole curve of the bay, the fishing boats coming in, the town huddled against the hill like it was trying to stay warm.

His father had been a keeper here, briefly, before the automation came and then the decommissioning. Tomas had been six. He remembered the smell of kerosene and the sound of the foghorn through the floor, a sound he had felt more than heard. He remembered his father's hands, always a little stained, always steady.

He did not know yet what he would do with the place. Fix the roof, certainly. Scrape the rust. Maybe nothing more than that. But he stood in the lamp room until the boats were all in and the light went amber over the water, and he understood that he had not bought a building. He had bought a way of looking out.

The bookshop had been closed for three winters when Ines inherited it, along with a ledger written in her aunt's slanting hand and a cat that nobody had mentioned. She arrived on a Thursday in late October with two suitcases and the address on a folded envelope, and she stood across the street for a long time, watching rain run down the painted letters, before she crossed. The door stuck. It had probably always stuck.

Inside, the shelves leaned toward each other like old friends sharing a secret, and the air smelled of paper and cold dust and, faintly, of oranges. She walked the narrow aisles slowly, reading spines. Poetry near the window. Maps in the back. A shelf of children's books at knee height, their corners soft from handling. The cat followed her at a distance, pretending not to.

Her aunt had run the place for forty years, and Ines had visited once, at nine, and been allowed to choose any book she wanted. She remembered the weight of the choice more than the book itself. She remembered her aunt's fingers, ink-smudged, tapping a spine and saying, not that one, you'll want that one later.

She did not know yet what she would do with the shop. Sweep, certainly. Fix the door. Perhaps nothing more than that. But she sat on the step-ladder until the streetlights came on and the rain turned silver in the window, and she understood that she had not inherited a business. She had inherited a place where people came to be found.

The first customer came on a Tuesday, before Ines had decided anything. She had swept twice and fixed the door with a wedge of folded cardboard, and the sign in the window still said CLOSED, but the man pushed inside anyway, shaking rain from a coat that had seen better decades. He did not seem surprised to find her there. He nodded as if she were expected, and went straight to the shelf of maps in the back.

She watched him from behind the counter, pretending to read the ledger. He pulled down an atlas so old its cover had gone the color of tea, opened it on the table by the window, and stood over it for nearly an hour, turning pages with the flat of his palm. The cat sat on the far end of the table and watched too.

When he finally came to the counter, he did not have the atlas. He had a slim paperback of poems she had not noticed him take. He set down coins from a pocket without asking the price, and the amount, when she counted it later, was exactly right.

"Your aunt kept the Norwegian coast on the third shelf," he said. "It's on the second now."

"I haven't moved anything," Ines said.

He considered this. "Then she did. Near the end." He put the book in his coat. "She said someone would come."

After he left, Ines climbed the step-ladder and found the coast, and behind it, tucked flat against the wall, an envelope with her name on it.

The envelope was not sealed. That was the first thing Ines noticed, and it bothered her more than it should have. Her aunt had been a woman who sealed things: jars, letters, the argument about the shop that had ended with Ines leaving for the city at nineteen and not coming back for eleven years. An unsealed envelope felt like a door left open on purpose.

Inside was a single sheet of the shop's own paper, the letterhead faded to a ghost of blue. The handwriting was smaller than Ines remembered, cramped toward the bottom as if her aunt had run out of room and refused to start a second page.

*You will want to sell it. Don't, at least not before the spring. The customers know the shelves better than you do, and they will tell you what they need if you let them stand and look. Marek will come first. He always comes first. Charge him nothing for the poems; he has paid for them a hundred times over. The ledger in the drawer is wrong in three places. I left it wrong so you would find them. The cat is called Hume and is not for sale.*

There was no signature. There did not need to be.

Ines stood on the ladder a long time, the rain loud against the window, the cat watching her with the patience of something that had already read the letter. Then she climbed down, went to the door, and turned the sign around.

## Hop 8

Extraction: bare/strict; original rule preserved: True.

Marek came at ten past nine, as the letter had promised, shaking water from a coat that had been brown once and was now the color of wet bark. He did not look surprised to see Ines behind the counter. He nodded at her the way one nods at weather.

"The Herbert," he said. "She keeps it on the second shelf, behind the atlases."

It was there. Ines found it by feel more than sight, a slim grey spine wedged where no customer would think to look. She set it on the counter and did not reach for the ledger.

"How much," Marek said, though he was already turning to the page he wanted, and she understood that it was not a question but a ritual, a thing said so that it could be refused.

"Nothing."

He read for a while, standing, his lips moving slightly. The cat came out from under the poetry table and sat on his boot. Outside, the rain thinned to something that was not quite mist.

"She told you about the ledger," he said, not looking up.

"Three places."

"Four." He closed the book and slid it back across to her. "But she only knew about three. The fourth is mine. April, nineteen years ago. I paid her for this book and she never wrote it down, so I've been paying her back ever since."

Ines put the Herbert behind the atlases. She would look for the fourth error later, or she would not. The shelves were beginning to tell her things.

The woman with the umbrella came on Thursday, though no letter had promised her. Ines knew her anyway, the way she was beginning to know things in this shop: by the angle at which the door was held open, by the pause before the bell.

"You're the niece," the woman said. It was not quite accurate. Ines let it stand.

"Rilke," the woman said. "The German, not the translation. She had it in the back."

The back was a room the size of a confessional, lined with boxes that had never been unpacked. Ines had not gone in yet. She went in now, and the smell of it was paper and cold and something floral that had lasted longer than it had any right to. The Rilke was in the third box, under a stack of railway timetables from a country that no longer existed. Someone had folded a tram ticket into it, at the ninth elegy.

She brought it out. The woman took it without opening it and held it against her coat.

"She told you about the ledger."

"Everyone tells me about the ledger."

The woman almost smiled. "I'm not in it. I never bought anything. I only borrowed." She set the Rilke down again, ticket and all. "I'll bring it back next month. That's how it was."

After she left, Ines opened the ledger for the first time and turned past the entries to the blank pages at the end. There were more of them than she had expected. The shop, it seemed, had been keeping room.

The piano tuner came on a Monday, which Ines had already learned was the day the shop belonged to no one in particular. He did not knock. He stood on the step with his case in both hands, as if he had been sent for and was waiting to be told he had the wrong address.

"There isn't a piano," Ines said.

"There was," he said. "Upstairs. She sold it in the winter. I'm not here for the piano."

He wanted the bench. She had not known there was a bench, but he described it so exactly—the hinge that caught, the lid that had to be lifted from the left—that she went up and found it under a dust sheet in the room with the sloped ceiling. Inside, instead of sheet music, there were letters, tied in bundles by year. The oldest was from before Ines was born.

She carried the bench down herself. He did not offer to help, and she understood that this was a kind of courtesy.

"You'll want to read those," he said, when she set it before him.

"Are they mine to read?"

He considered the question longer than she expected. "They're the shop's," he said. "Which is the same thing now, or will be."

He left the letters. He took only the bench, tilting it through the doorway with the ease of someone who had done it before. The bell rang once behind him, then settled.

Ins sat on the floor where the bench had been and untied the first bundle, and the paper made the sound of something being let out.

The letters were not addressed to anyone Ines recognized. They began, most of them, with a single initial and a dash, as though the writer had assumed the reader would know who was meant. The handwriting changed across the years, slanting first one way and then the other, but the paper stayed the same: thin, blue-lined, torn from the same kind of pad.

She read the first one twice. It described a window. Not the shop's window, she thought, but one somewhere else, where the light came in wrong in the afternoons and had to be corrected with a curtain. The writer had measured the curtain three times and still gotten it short. There was no signature.

By the fourth letter Ines understood that they were all about rooms. A kitchen with a step down that everyone forgot. A landing where a clock had been moved and the wall showed its pale shape for years. The writer went from house to house, it seemed, and wrote about what each one refused to give up.

She found the shop in the eleventh letter. The bell that rang once and then settled. The room with the sloped ceiling, empty then, waiting for something to be carried into it.

Ines set the bundle down and listened. Above her, the floorboards made the small adjustments they made when no one was on them. She thought of the piano tuner tilting the bench through the door, and of what he had said—the shop's, which is the same thing now—and she reached for the next year.

She did not turn it. Not that night. But she took a knife from the kitchen and began, very carefully, to score the paint around its edge, the way one loosens a lid that has been closed too long.

That was the word the writer had used. Company.

Ines turned the wheel a quarter turn. It resisted, then gave, with a small dry sound like a knuckle cracking. Nothing happened. She waited. The courtyard was silent except for a pigeon on the gutter.

The seventh letter did not come. Ines waited for it the way you wait for a step on the stair that has always come at the same hour, and when the hour passed she felt the absence more sharply than she had felt any of the arrivals.

Ines put her other hand flat against the wood. It was warm. Warmer than the stairwell, warmer than her palm.

Someone, she understood, had been on the other side of it a moment ago.

The door opened inward on a breath of air that was not stale. That was the first wrong thing. Eleven years of a locked room should have smelled of dust and mouse and forgotten damp, and instead it smelled faintly of coffee, of soap, of a window recently shut.

The room was small and square and lived in. A cot along the far wall, blanket folded with a neatness that felt like an apology. A table under the sloped ceiling with a candle stub, a saucer of pins, a stack of paper cut to the same size as the letters. A chair pushed back at an angle, as though someone had stood up quickly and not sat down again.

And on the floorboards, running in a thin bright line from the table to the far wall, a strip of light. She followed it with her eyes to a knot in the wood where the light came through, and beneath it, worn pale by years of a body lying pressed close, a shallow hollow the shape of a shoulder and a cheek.

She knelt. She put her eye to the knothole.

Below her was the back room of the shop, her lamp still burning on the shelf where she left it every night, the counter, the door she locked, the stool where she sat to mend the account books. Everything she did alone. Everything she had believed no one saw.

Behind her, on the stairs, a board took a weight and held its breath.

Ines did not turn around. She said, quietly, to the floor, "You can come in. It's your room."
