## Hop 1

The tide came in an hour early. Nobody in the village noticed at first, because the harbor clock had been running slow since the winter storms, and most people trusted the clock more than the sea. Only old Marta, who had gathered mussels on the flats every morning for forty years, felt something was wrong. She was halfway to the rocks when the water began curling around her ankles, cold and insistent, much sooner than it should have. She turned back, her basket only half full, and watched the channel fill with a speed that made her uneasy. By the time she reached the seawall, the fishing boats were already lifting off the mud, their ropes tightening like the strings of an instrument being tuned. Children who had been digging for crabs came running, shrieking with delight rather than fear, their buckets sloshing. Marta went straight to the harbormaster's hut and knocked until he opened the door, still holding his morning coffee. She told him the sea was ahead of itself. He laughed, then glanced at his tide tables, then at the water, and stopped laughing. They stood together on the wall as the gulls wheeled overhead, calling in sharp, broken voices. Something far out in the grey distance had shifted, a current or a sandbar or something neither of them could name. That afternoon, the whole village gathered on the headland to watch, and for the first time in years, they set their watches by the waves instead.

## Hop 2

The lighthouse keeper's daughter found the bottle on a Tuesday, wedged between two stones at the base of the cliff where the kelp gathered in thick, rubbery tangles. It was green glass, clouded by salt, and sealed with wax the color of old blood. Inside, curled tight, was a sheet of paper. She did not open it right away. Instead she carried it up the two hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room, where her father was polishing the great lens with a cloth soft as a rabbit's ear. He looked at the bottle for a long time without speaking. Then he set down his cloth and said that bottles like that one used to wash ashore when he was a boy, sent by sailors who wanted someone, anyone, to know they had existed. Most of them held names and dates. A few held love letters. One, he said, had held nothing but a single pressed flower, which his mother had kept in her Bible until the day she died. The girl broke the wax with her thumbnail. The paper unrolled reluctantly, brittle at the edges, and the ink had faded to the pale brown of weak tea. It was a map, hand-drawn, showing their own stretch of coast with a small cross marked on the northern headland. Beneath it, in careful letters, someone had written: for whoever keeps the light. Her father went very quiet. That evening, as the beam swept out across the darkening water, they packed a lantern and a shovel and began to walk north.

## Hop 3

The northern headland was farther than it looked from the lamp room, and by the time they reached it the moon had risen, thin and sharp as a fingernail paring. The wind came hard off the water, flattening the grass and pulling at the lantern flame until the girl cupped her hand around the glass to shelter it. Her father walked ahead with the shovel over his shoulder, counting paces under his breath the way he counted the turns of the clockwork that drove the lens. The map showed the cross set between a crooked hawthorn and a stone that looked, in the drawing, like a sleeping dog. They found the hawthorn first, bent almost double by a century of gales. The stone took longer. It was half buried in bracken, but when the lantern light fell across it, the girl laughed out loud, because it truly did look like a dog curled with its nose tucked under its tail. Her father began to dig. The soil was thin and full of roots, and twice the shovel rang against rock. The girl held the lantern steady and watched his breath smoke in the cold air. At last the blade struck something that was not stone. It was a tin box, rusted nearly through, no bigger than a loaf of bread. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a brass key and a photograph of a woman standing on the lighthouse gallery, one hand raised as if to wave. Her father stared at it for a long moment. Then, very softly, he said his mother's name.

## Hop 10

The sapling arrived on the supply boat two weeks later, its roots bundled in sacking and its thin branches tied with twine. The boatman handed it up without a word, as though he had carried stranger things across the strait, and the girl held it against her chest the whole way up the path. Her father had already mixed seaweed and ash into the soil by the door. Together they lowered the tree into the hole, and he pressed the earth around it with his palms, slowly, the way he used to smooth the blankets over her when she was small. That night a gale came in from the west. The girl lay awake listening to the shutters bang and imagined the sapling snapped in two, its roots torn loose and flung into the sea. At first light she ran outside barefoot. The tree was bent nearly to the ground, but it held. Her father had driven a stake beside it sometime in the dark and bound the trunk with strips of old sailcloth. He was sitting on the step with his coat still wet, watching the branches shiver. Through the long summer she carried water to it from the rain barrel, and he checked the bindings every evening before he climbed the tower. In autumn it dropped its few leaves onto the stones. The next spring, when the mainland turned green across the water, the girl found a single white blossom on the lowest branch. She did not pick it. She went inside, took the tin from the shelf, and set it on the table beside her father's cup.

## Hop 20

The minister nodded as though this settled something, and he did not ask anything more. After he had gone, the girl sat on the step and watched the rowan move in the wind. She tried to remember her mother saying those words and could not, and the not-remembering frightened her more than the storm had. That night she asked her father what else her mother had said. He was quiet so long that she thought he had not heard. Then he set down his pipe and began, haltingly, to tell her: how her mother sang to the hens so they would lay, how she kept a sprig of rowan above the door of the byre, how she had once walked three miles in the rain to return a borrowed spoon. The stories came slowly at first, then faster, as if they had been waiting behind a door he had finally opened. The girl listened until the lamp burned low. In August the berries came, small and hard and green, clustered at the tips of the thin branches. By September they had turned a red so bright it seemed to hum against the grey sky. The birds found them before anyone else did, a pair of blackbirds that quarrelled in the grass below. Her father did not chase them off. 'Let them have some,' he said. 'That's what they're for.' The girl cut a single sprig before the birds took the rest, tied it with a length of the net twine, and hung it above the kitchen door, where it stayed all winter.

## Hop 30

Snow came late that year, not until the week after the new year, and it fell so softly in the night that the girl woke to a silence she did not recognise. The sea was still there, grumbling at the foot of the cliff, but everything else had gone quiet under the white. She dressed properly this time, because her grandmother was already at the stove and would have had something to say, and then she went out with a crust of bread in her pocket for the birds. The ring of stones had become a ring of small white hills. Inside it the rowan stood black and thin, each branch lined along its top with a narrow ridge of snow, as if someone had drawn over it with chalk. She brushed a little from the lowest twig and found the buds still there, hard and patient. A robin came down onto the nearest stone and watched her with one eye. She broke the crust and scattered it, and the robin waited until she had stepped back before it took any. Her brother came out later with the shovel to clear the path to the byre, and on his way back he stopped at the stones and knocked the heaviest snow from the branches so they would not break. He did it carefully, one branch at a time, the way their father mended nets. In the jotter that night, beside the brown ghosts of the October leaves, she wrote: everybody is looking after the tree now, even the ones who pretend they are not.

## Hop 40

Autumn arrived all at once, in a single night of wind that stripped the rowan and scattered its berries across the path like beads from a broken string. The girl swept them into the flowerbed so her grandmother would not have to see them trodden into the stones, though her grandmother no longer came to the door. The bed stayed in the front room. A nurse visited on Tuesdays and Fridays, a quiet woman with cold hands who always wiped her shoes twice on the mat and called the girl pet. Her brother came home in September, browner and taller, with a cut across one palm he would not explain. He stood in the doorway of the front room for a long time before he went in. Afterwards he walked out to the garden and stood under the bare rowan, and the girl let him be. That evening he mended the gate, which had sagged on its hinges since spring, and nobody had asked him to. The thrushes had gone by then. Their nest in the hawthorn sat empty, neat and round, lined with mud and grass the way their mother's had been, and the girl checked it every morning anyway. In October her grandmother asked for the window to be opened, though the air was sharp. She wanted to hear the garden, she said. The girl opened it and tucked the blanket higher. Outside, a blackbird was turning over leaves beneath the tree, patient, searching for the last of the berries. Listen, her grandmother said. Still here. The girl sat down beside the bed and listened.

## Hop 50

The reply came in September, when the beans had begun to fatten in their pods and the leaves at the bottom of the wigwam were turning yellow at the edges. It was a small parcel this time, wrapped in brown paper and tied with garden twine, and her mother left it on the kitchen table beside the notebook without a word. Inside was a letter folded into quarters and a tin that had once held peppermints. The letter was short. Her cousin wrote that the three beans had gone into a pot on the windowsill, because the wind off the sea was too strong for canes, and that two of them had come up. The second had flowered late, in August, but it had flowered. At the bottom, in the same careful writing, her cousin had added that she had started keeping a notebook too. The girl opened the tin. It was full of seeds she did not recognise: flat and papery, round and black, some striped like tiny shells. Each kind was folded into a twist of paper with a name pencilled on it. Marigold. Nasturtium. Sweet pea. Something she could not read. She sat at the table for a long time, turning the twists over in her fingers, while the kettle ticked as it cooled. Then she went outside with a basket and began to pick the fattest pods, splitting one with her thumbnail to check the beans inside were speckled and dry. She would save the best for next year. She would save some for Aberdeen.

## Hop 56

By August the row had grown taller than the girl herself, and the heads turned each morning to follow the sun over the chimneys. Neighbours slowed on their way to the bus to look, and an old man from the end of the street stopped one evening to say he had not seen the like since before the war. The girl did not know which war he meant and did not ask. She only nodded and went on filling the watering can at the rain butt, carrying it slopping down the path with both hands. The crooked one at the end had caught up a little but never quite straightened, and it bloomed last of all, a small bright face no wider than a saucer, still leaning toward the fence. Her mother came out to see it in her slippers, a tea towel over one shoulder, and stood a long time with her arms folded. Then she said, quite suddenly, that her own mother had always saved the seeds from the smallest flower, because the smallest one had worked hardest to be there. The girl had not heard this before. In September she cut the heads and hung them in the shed to dry, and when they were ready she rubbed the seeds loose into a jam jar with her thumbs. She kept back twenty from the little crooked flower and folded them into an envelope. On the front, in her careful best handwriting, she wrote her cousin's address in the north, and underneath, smaller, she added: plant these first.

## Hop 57

The envelope reached the north on a wet Tuesday in October, soft at the corners from the rain. Her cousin Callum, who was nine and did not much care for letters, turned it over twice before his grandmother told him to open it at the table and not on the stairs. Twenty grey-striped seeds slid out onto the oilcloth. He counted them, then counted them again, and read the small words at the bottom aloud: plant these first. His grandmother laughed and said that was her sister's saying, and her mother's before that, and she had not thought anyone still remembered it. Callum wanted to plant them straight away, but she explained that the ground up here was already cold and the frost would take them. So he kept the envelope in the drawer of his bedside table all winter, beside a torch and a broken compass, and some nights he took it out just to feel the seeds shift inside the paper. In April, when the light came back and the wind off the hills lost its edge, he chose the most sheltered corner of the yard, against the south wall where the bins used to stand. He dug with a spoon because the trowel was too big for his hands, pressed each seed down with one finger, and watered them from a milk bottle. Then he fetched a sheet of paper and a pencil, and, slowly, in his own careful best handwriting, he began a letter back to the south.

## Hop 58

The letter took him three evenings to finish, because he kept crossing things out and starting again on a clean sheet. He told his cousin about the south wall and the spoon and the milk bottle, and he drew a map of the yard with an arrow pointing to the corner where the seeds were sleeping. He asked whether her sunflowers had grown taller than the fence, and whether she had saved any seeds of her own, and whether it was true that you had to plant them first or they would not come up at all. His grandmother said nothing about the spelling, only folded the pages neatly and found him a stamp with a lighthouse on it. For two weeks nothing happened in the corner. Callum checked it every morning before school, crouching in his pyjamas with his coat thrown over the top, and every morning the soil looked exactly the same, dark and patient and closed. Then, on a Saturday when the rain had finally stopped, he found a single pale loop pushing up through the earth, bent over like someone tying a shoelace. By the evening there were four. By the end of the week there were eleven, and he stopped counting because his grandmother said it was bad luck to count green things before they were grown. The reply from the south arrived in May. Inside, folded around a photograph of a very tall yellow flower, was a single line in his cousin's round handwriting: now you have to save some for someone else.

## Hop 59

Callum read the line four times before he understood it, and then he went out to the corner and stood looking at the sunflowers as if they had been listening. They were taller than him by midsummer, taller than his grandmother by August, and their heavy faces turned slowly across the yard every day, following the light from the kitchen window to the shed. When the petals began to curl and brown, his grandmother showed him how to tie paper bags over the heads so the birds would not take everything. In September they cut the stalks together, and he carried the flower heads into the kitchen one at a time, holding them in both arms like sleeping animals. They spread newspaper across the table and sat rubbing the seeds loose with their thumbs until their fingers were black and striped. There were far more than he had expected, hundreds of them, rattling into the biscuit tin with a sound like rain on a roof. His grandmother kept a handful for the birds in winter and let him choose the rest. He thought for a long time about who should have them. In the end he counted out twenty, even though it was probably bad luck, and folded them into an envelope with a map of the yard and an arrow pointing to the south wall. On the front he wrote the address of the boy next door, who had only moved in that summer and had no garden at all. Then, underneath the map, he copied out the line exactly.

## Hop 60

The boy next door was called Idris, and he did not open the envelope for three days. Callum knew this because he watched it sitting on the windowsill across the fence, pale against the glass, while the weather turned and the first leaves came down in the lane. On the fourth morning it was gone. Nothing happened after that for a long time. Winter came in hard and grey, and his grandmother scattered the saved seeds on the bird table, where sparrows argued over them and a single robin waited on the gatepost for its turn. Callum stopped thinking about the envelope, or told himself he had. Then, in April, he heard scraping on the other side of the fence and climbed onto the water butt to look. Idris was kneeling on the concrete with a spoon, digging into a cracked plastic tub he had filled with soil from somewhere. Beside him lay the map, soft now from folding, weighted down with a stone. He had drawn his own arrow on the back of it, pointing to the one patch of his yard that caught the sun in the afternoon. He looked up and saw Callum watching, and neither of them said anything for a moment. Then Idris held up the envelope and shook it, and Callum heard the small dry sound of seeds still inside. Twenty, Idris said. I counted. Callum climbed down and went to find his grandmother's trowel, and when he passed it over the fence, Idris read the line aloud, carefully, the way you say something you have practised.
