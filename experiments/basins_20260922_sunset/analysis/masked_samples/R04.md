## Hop 1

The sun was setting behind the hills when Mara reached the abandoned railway platform. She had walked there every evening for a week, though no train had stopped in the village for thirty years. Tonight, a small blue suitcase waited beside the bench.

It was the kind her father had carried when he left for the coast. Mara recognized the cracked handle and the faded sticker of a lighthouse. She knelt to touch it, then noticed an envelope tucked beneath the latch. Her name was written across it in his careful, slanting hand.

Inside was a single sheet of paper. He had not explained where he had gone or why he had stayed away. Instead, he had written about a summer afternoon when she was seven, when they had missed the last train and walked home through the fields. She remembered complaining about the distance. He remembered her stopping to rescue a beetle from a puddle.

At the bottom of the page, he had written, ‘I should have come back sooner.’

Mara sat on the bench until the first stars appeared. The suitcase held only a wool scarf, a photograph of the two of them, and a jar of seashells. None of it answered the questions she had carried for years. Yet as the platform darkened, she wrapped the scarf around her shoulders and opened the jar. She poured a few shells into her palm, listening as they clicked together, and decided she would write to him in the morning.

## Hop 2

In the morning, Mara placed a sheet of paper on the kitchen table. She wrote her father’s name, then stopped. She knew how to tell him that the scarf was warm and that the shells smelled faintly of salt. She did not know how to fit thirty years of silence into an envelope.

Outside, rain gathered in the gutters. Mara made tea and took out the photograph. She was missing a front tooth, and her father had one hand on her shoulder. Behind them stood a painted sign for the village fair. She remembered the day now: he had won the blue suitcase in a raffle and let her carry it home, though it was nearly as big as she was.

She began again. ‘I found what you left at the station.’ After a while, she added, ‘I remember the beetle, too.’

The rest came slowly. She told him about her mother’s garden, the work she did at the library, and the dog that had adopted her last winter. Then she wrote what she had meant to say first: she was angry he had gone. She had missed him anyway.

By noon, the rain had stopped. Mara folded the letter and addressed it to the return address on his envelope, a street near the coast. At the postbox, she held it for a moment before letting it fall. Then she walked home by the long path through the fields, watching for puddles.

## Hop 3

For three weeks, Mara listened for the post each morning. She told herself she was waiting for a book she had ordered for the library, but when its padded envelope arrived, she left it unopened on the hall table.

Her father’s letter came on a Thursday. The envelope was thin. She carried it into the kitchen and set it beside the blue suitcase, which she had brought down from the attic after writing to him. Its clasp had rusted, and one wheel no longer turned.

He thanked her for writing. He said he was sorry, though he did not try to explain the years away. He remembered the fair, too, and how she had refused to let him help with the suitcase, stopping every few steps to change hands. At the bottom of the page, he had written his telephone number. She read the letter twice, then put it back in its envelope.

That afternoon, Mara took the suitcase to a repair shop. The man behind the counter turned the broken wheel with his thumb and said he might be able to find a match. She left it with him.

At home, she stood by the telephone for a while. Then she went outside to bring in the washing before the clouds reached the garden. She folded the sheets, made supper, and washed her plate. Afterward, she called the number.

A man answered on the third ring. Mara looked out at the darkening fields and said, ‘Hello, Dad.’

## Hop 10

The clock in the hallway had stopped at twenty past six. Mara noticed it as she carried the last box to the car, though she had passed beneath it all morning without looking up. Her father used to wind it on Sundays, standing on a chair because he said the stool was unsteady.

She set the box on the passenger seat. Inside were three dictionaries, a packet of birthday candles, and the little brass bell he had rung when supper was ready. She had meant to leave the bell for the new owners. At the last moment, she had wrapped it in a tea towel.

Across the street, Mrs. Vale was cutting back her roses. She raised a hand but did not come over. Mara was grateful for that. They had already said goodbye beside the bins, where Mrs. Vale had pressed a paper bag of plums into her arms.

Mara went back inside for the cleaning cloths. The rooms smelled faintly of polish and the rain that had come through the open windows. In the hallway she took out her phone to photograph the stopped clock, then lowered it. The movers would arrive for it tomorrow. Someone would wind it again.

She checked the kitchen taps and closed the window above the sink. On the sill lay a single plum, smaller than the others, with a soft spot near its stem. She ate around the bruise while standing at the counter.

Before locking the front door, she went into the hallway and moved the clock’s hands forward by one minute.

## Hop 20

By Sunday, the leaves had dried enough to lift from the gate with a broom. Lena swept them into a pile beside the wall, where the wind immediately began to loosen it. She fetched a bucket and carried them to the compost bin.

Her sister arrived just after two, bringing a small tin of biscuits. They made tea before looking at the keys. The one with the red thread was shorter than the others, with a shallow notch along one side. Her sister turned it over in her palm.

“I think this was for the shed,” she said.

The shed had been locked for as long as Lena could remember. Its door had swollen in the damp, and a blackberry stem had grown across the handle. They took a pair of gloves outside. Lena held the stem aside while her sister put the key in the lock. It turned, but the door would not move until they both pulled.

Inside were empty paint tins, a broken folding chair, and a length of wood propped against the back wall. Her sister found a box of screws on a shelf. Lena noticed pencil marks on the wall near it, each beside a date. The highest mark was too faint to read.

They did not stay long. Lena put the screws in her coat pocket and pulled the door closed. Back in the kitchen, her sister wrote “shed” on a scrap of paper and laid it beneath the key. Lena opened the notebook and added a note about the swollen door.

## Hop 30

On Monday morning, Elena found a blue notebook on the seat beside her as the train pulled into the station. Its cover was worn, and a paper bookmark stuck out near the middle. She picked it up, hoping to find a name inside, but the pages held only sketches of buildings and brief notes about windows, doors, and rooflines.

At the station office, the clerk told her lost property would not open until noon. Elena had a meeting across town, so she left the notebook with him and wrote down her phone number in case its owner called.

That evening, a man named Ravi rang to thank her. He explained that the notebook contained ideas for a mural he was planning with students at a local school. He had been drawing places the children passed every day: the bakery, the library, and the narrow bridge over the canal. Without the notebook, he would have had trouble remembering which details they had chosen together.

Elena asked when the mural would be finished. Ravi laughed and said they had not started painting yet. The students were still deciding what belonged in the background. He invited her to visit once the work was underway.

Two weeks later, Elena stopped by the school on her lunch break. A teacher led her to a courtyard wall covered in bright outlines. Several students were painting the bridge while Ravi helped another mix a shade of green. Near the bottom, someone had added a small blue notebook on a train seat. Elena spotted it before Ravi pointed it out, and smiled.

## Hop 40

The next box arrived on Monday. It had once held oranges, and the baker set it beneath the window with a stack of clean cards inside. By lunchtime, someone had written a recipe for soup on the first one. By closing, there were three more.

Imani liked reading them while her grandfather chose his bread. Some were careful, with measurements and oven temperatures. Others said things like Cook until it smells right, or Add enough lemon to make everyone ask what’s in it.

One rainy afternoon, she found a card in Elias’s handwriting. It described how to mend a torn apron pocket with a scrap of cloth. At the bottom he had added, Not a recipe, but useful when you carry too many things.

The baker left it in the box.

Soon the cards included instructions for growing mint on a windowsill, getting candle wax out of a tablecloth, and teaching a dog to wait by the door. Imani’s grandfather contributed one for whistling through a blade of grass. It took him three attempts to explain it clearly, and another four to demonstrate it.

In spring, the baker tied the oldest cards into bundles with string. He put them on a shelf where anyone could borrow one and bring it back. Imani watched a woman take the jam-pocket card, now soft at the edges.

‘Do you think she’ll put a tray underneath?’ Imani asked.

‘If she reads the whole thing,’ her grandfather said.

The woman paused at the door, turned the card over, and smiled. Then she went back to ask the baker for pastry scraps.

## Hop 50

The telephone rang so long that Imani thought Nabil might be out. Then her grandfather’s face brightened.

“Are you sitting down?” he asked. “We found the marble.”

Imani could hear a voice through the receiver, though not the words. Her grandfather laughed and held out the phone.

“Nabil wants to tell you something.”

Imani pressed the receiver to her ear. Nabil’s voice was scratchy and warm. “I gave that marble to your grandfather because he’d lost his favorite one. Mine had a yellow streak inside the glass. Does it still?”

She lifted it toward the window. In the morning light, the streak shone like a thin ribbon. “It does.”

“Then tell him I was right. He didn’t lose it again.”

Her grandfather shook his head when she repeated this. “Not for long, anyway.”

Before they hung up, Nabil promised to visit on Saturday. He asked whether the bakery still made sesame rings, and her grandfather said he would have a fresh batch waiting.

Afterward, Imani set the marble on the counter beside Sami’s drawing. A customer came in for a loaf, and her grandfather wrapped it in paper. He seemed about to put the drawing away, but instead he propped it against the till.

“Will Nabil want the marble back?” Imani asked.

“I don’t think so. But we can ask him.”

He handed her a sesame ring, still warm enough to soften the paper bag. Imani took a bite and looked at the three boys by the fence. On Saturday, she would get to meet one of them.

## Hop 56

The next morning, Imani and Sami found the red door between a fruit seller’s stall and a shop full of clocks. Sami knocked while Imani held the notebook open to a clean page.

A woman answered. When they told her about the school bell, she looked surprised.

“My father brought home all sorts of things to repair,” she said. “Come in. We can look in his workshop.”

The workshop was a small room at the back of the building. Jars of screws lined the shelves, and tools hung above a wooden bench. The woman opened a cupboard and lifted out a box of hinges, then another filled with brass handles. At the bottom of the third box lay a bell wrapped in a square of cloth.

Imani knew it by its narrow handle and rounded lip. A thin crack ran up one side.

“Father tried to mend it,” the woman said, turning it carefully in her hands. “He never found a way to make it ring properly again.”

Sami asked if they could hear it. She gave the bell a gentle shake. It made a soft, uneven note.

“It still has a voice,” Imani said.

The woman smiled. “Would you take it to the old school? I think he meant to return it.”

They carried it to the bakery first. Imani’s grandfather cleared a place for it beside the notebook, where everyone could see what they had found. Imani drew the crack, then wrote down the woman’s name and her father’s. She left one last blank line for the day the bell went home.

## Hop 57

By noon, three people had come into the bakery to see the bell. The fruit seller remembered hearing it on her first day at the old school. A man buying bread said its handle had always been crooked, even before it cracked. Imani added both memories to her notebook.

After the lunch rush, Grandfather wrapped the bell in a clean tea towel. He walked with Imani and Sami up the hill, past the playground and through the school’s iron gate. The building had been empty for years, but the caretaker still swept the steps and kept a key to the front door.

He listened as they explained where they had found the bell. Then he led them down a corridor to a room with tall windows. A hook hung beside the doorway.

“That’s where it used to go,” he said.

Sami stood on a chair and held the bell near the hook. Its handle fit easily, but he waited for the caretaker to lift it into place. For a moment, nobody spoke.

“Should we try it?” Imani asked.

The caretaker nodded. Imani reached up and gave the bell one careful shake. Its note wavered through the empty room, quiet but clear enough to make Sami grin.

That evening, Imani returned to the bakery and filled in the last line of her notebook. Beneath the date, she wrote: The bell is back at school. Then she left the notebook open so Grandfather could read it.

## Hop 58

The next morning, Imani found a folded note beside the bakery till. The caretaker had written to say that the school bell had brought back more memories than he expected. Would she and Sami come by after breakfast?

They carried a paper bag of rolls up the hill. At the school gate, they met the fruit seller, who was waiting with a photograph. It showed a line of children beneath the tall windows. Though the picture was faded, Imani could make out the bell beside the doorway.

“My mother took this on my first day,” the fruit seller said. “I thought the bell sounded enormous.”

Inside, the caretaker had set a small table under the windows. The man who had noticed the crooked handle arrived with another photograph, this one of a school play. Soon everyone was pointing out faces and trying to remember names.

Sami drew the bell as it hung on its hook. Imani copied the names onto fresh pages in her notebook, leaving space for stories beside each one. When the caretaker offered to keep the notebook at the school, she hesitated. She had carried it everywhere since finding the bell.

“You can take it home when you want,” he said. “But people might like to add to it.”

Imani placed it on the table. The fruit seller wrote the first new memory, pressing hard enough to leave marks on the next page.

Before they left, the caretaker rang the bell once. From the playground below came the sound of children calling to one another. For a moment, the two sounds seemed to belong together.

## Hop 59

On Saturday, Imani returned to the school with Sami and a tin of biscuits. She had meant to check on the notebook, but found three people waiting outside the gate. Each had brought something: a class photograph, a report card, and a small wooden ruler with a name scratched into its edge.

The caretaker let them in. On the table beneath the windows, the notebook lay open to a page Imani had left blank. Someone had written about a winter when snow covered the playground and lessons moved into the bakery because its ovens kept the room warm.

“Was that your bakery?” Sami asked.

“My grandfather’s,” Imani said. She read the story again, trying to picture desks between the flour sacks.

They spent the morning matching names to faces. When a visitor could not remember the date of a photograph, the others searched for clues: a coat, a haircut, the painted sign above the classroom door. Sami drew each object as they found it.

Near lunchtime, the fruit seller arrived with an elderly woman on his arm. She stopped beneath the bell and smiled.

“I used to ring that,” she said. “Only when the teacher was away.”

Everyone laughed, and Imani gave her the notebook. The woman wrote slowly. When she finished, she pointed to an empty space beside her story and asked Sami to draw the bell there.

On the way home, Imani looked back at the school. More people were coming up the hill, carrying photographs in their hands.

## Hop 60

By the following Saturday, the caretaker had found a second table for the schoolroom. People kept arriving with things they thought someone else might recognize: a brass button, a spelling prize, a photograph so faded that only the bell above the door was clear.

Imani set out the biscuits while Sami arranged the objects in rows. Beside each one, he placed a slip of paper for its owner’s name and whatever else they knew.

A man in a blue cap picked up the brass button. “That’s from the caretaker’s old coat,” he said. “He used to lend it to anyone who forgot theirs.”

The caretaker, standing by the stove, shook his head. “It was too big for all of you.”

“Which was why we liked it,” the man replied.

Imani wrote the exchange in the notebook. Across the room, two sisters were arguing cheerfully over the year of the spelling prize. One remembered the teacher who had awarded it; the other remembered being jealous. Between them, they settled on a date.

In the afternoon, the elderly woman returned with a folded sheet of paper. It was a list of songs the children had sung while waiting for the bakery ovens to warm the room. She could recall only the first line of one song, but three visitors knew the rest. They sang it softly, then again so Sami could write down the words.

When it was time to leave, Imani put the notebook in the cupboard. The table was still crowded with objects. This time, she left the slips of paper beside them.
