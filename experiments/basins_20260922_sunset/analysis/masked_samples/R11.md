## Hop 1

The sun was setting behind the hills when Mara reached the empty station. Its clock had stopped at half past four, though the evening train was due in minutes. She set her suitcase on the platform and listened. Somewhere beyond the tracks, a dog barked. From the station office came the dry rustle of paper.

The door stood ajar. Inside, a man in a railway uniform was sorting letters into neat piles. He looked up as Mara entered.

“Is the train running?” she asked.

“It will come,” he said, and returned to his work.

Mara waited by the doorway. Most of the envelopes were yellowed, their stamps faded. On top of one pile lay a letter addressed to her mother, at the house they had left twenty years ago. She recognized the careful handwriting of her grandfather, who had died that same winter.

“Where did you get that?” she asked.

The man glanced at the envelope. “It was never collected.”

Outside, a whistle sounded in the valley. Mara picked up the letter, but her thumb caught under the flap. She could open it now. She could carry it home and let her mother decide. The whistle sounded again, closer this time.

She placed the unopened letter in her coat pocket and hurried back to the platform. A single headlamp appeared around the bend. As the train slowed, she turned to thank the clerk, but the office was dark. Through its dusty window she could see a bare desk, an overturned chair, and no sign that anyone had been there that evening.

## Hop 2

By the time Mara reached her mother’s house, rain had begun to tap against the windows. She left her suitcase in the hall and found her mother at the kitchen table, shelling peas into a chipped blue bowl.

“I went through the old station,” Mara said.

Her mother’s hands stopped. “There hasn’t been a train through there in years.”

Mara took the letter from her pocket. The envelope had softened in the rain, but the address was still legible. Her mother stared at it for a long moment before wiping her hands on a towel.

“I thought he’d stopped writing,” she said.

Mara sat across from her. “You don’t have to open it.”

Her mother gave a small, surprised laugh. “I know.” She slid one finger beneath the flap.

Inside was a single page. Mara watched her mother read it once, then again. At last she passed it across the table. Her grandfather had written about the apple tree behind their old house: how its branches had survived a hard frost, how he hoped to bring them a basket of fruit in autumn. At the bottom, in cramped writing, he had added that he was sorry for the things he had said when they left.

“That’s all?” Mara asked gently.

Her mother nodded. She folded the page along its old creases and held it between her palms. Outside, the rain grew heavier. Neither of them spoke for a while. Then her mother pushed the bowl toward Mara, and together they finished shelling the peas.

## Hop 3

The next morning, Mara found her mother standing beneath the apple tree. Rain still clung to its leaves, and the grass soaked the hems of their trousers as Mara crossed the yard.

“I didn’t know you’d planted one here,” Mara said.

Her mother rested a hand against the trunk. “Your grandfather brought it as a sapling. The year you were born.”

Mara looked up. The branches were bare of fruit, though a few small apples lay in the wet grass. She picked one up and turned it in her palm. A bird had pecked a neat hole in its side.

“He never came back after that,” her mother said. “I told myself it was because he was too proud.”

“Maybe he was.” Mara slipped the apple into her coat pocket. “Maybe he didn’t know if he’d be welcome.”

Her mother said nothing. From the kitchen came the faint rattle of the loose window latch, a sound Mara remembered from childhood. She had offered to fix it yesterday, and her mother had told her not to bother.

They stood beneath the tree until the clouds began to thin. Then her mother pointed to a branch hanging low over the path.

“That one needs cutting back before winter.”

Mara went to the shed for the pruning shears. When she returned, her mother was gathering the fallen apples into the hem of her sweater. Some were bruised; a few were sound. She carried them inside to wash, while Mara steadied the branch with one hand and made the first cut.
