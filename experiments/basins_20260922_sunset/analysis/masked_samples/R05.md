## Hop 1

The tide came in an hour early. Marguerite noticed it first from the kitchen window, the way the water had already swallowed the flat gray stones where the gulls usually stood at this time of morning. She checked the almanac pinned beside the stove, ran her finger down the column of dates, and frowned. The numbers were right. The sea was wrong.

She pulled on her boots and walked down to the shingle. The air smelled of iodine and something sharper, like a struck match. Out past the breakwater, the water lay unusually still, a sheet of pewter without the small chop that normally ruffled it. The fishing boats had not gone out. She could see them at their moorings, bobbing in a slow, uneasy rhythm, and along the harbor wall a handful of men stood with their hands in their pockets, saying nothing.

Old Teodor was among them. He had fished these waters for sixty years and claimed he could feel weather in his knees before the radio announced it. When Marguerite reached him he did not turn, only nodded toward the horizon.

"Twice in my life," he said. "Once in fifty-three. Once the winter my brother drowned."

"What does it mean?"

He shrugged, but his jaw was tight. "Means the sea has changed its mind about something."

By noon the water had climbed the lower steps of the church. The children were kept indoors. Marguerite moved her mother's photographs to the upper shelf and waited, listening to the strange, patient lapping at the door.

## Hop 2

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Hollis would later think of as the last ordinary day. It came in a plain cream envelope with no return address, and the handwriting on the front was so familiar that his hand shook before his mind had caught up to why.

He set it on the kitchen table and made coffee he did not drink. Outside, the neighbor's sprinkler ticked across the lawn in its usual arc. A dog barked twice and stopped. Everything insisted on being normal.

The handwriting belonged to his sister. She had been dead for eleven years.

Hollis knew the loops of her capital H, the way she crossed her sevens like a European, the slight leftward lean that their mother had tried and failed to correct. He had kept every birthday card she ever sent him in a shoebox in the closet, and he did not need to take them out to compare.

When he finally opened it, the paper inside was a single sheet, folded once. There was no date. It said only: *I'm sorry it took so long. Meet me where we buried the tin.*

The tin. A biscuit tin, painted with faded roses, that they had filled with marbles and a plastic dinosaur and a note to their future selves, then sunk into the soft earth beneath the willow at their grandmother's farm. The farm had been sold in ninety-eight. The willow, for all he knew, was gone.

Hollis read the note four times. Then he took his keys from the hook by the door, and did not stop to lock it behind him.

## Hop 3

The drive took four hours, most of it on roads Hollis had not traveled since he was a boy. He did not turn on the radio. He watched the fields flatten and then rise again into the low hills of the county where his grandmother had lived, and he tried not to think about what he was doing, because thinking about it made it impossible.

The farm had a new name on the gate. Someone had painted the barn a hard modern red and put up a satellite dish. But the willow was still there, enormous now, its branches sweeping the ground like a woman gathering her skirts. He parked on the shoulder and walked across the field without asking anyone's permission.

The earth beneath the tree was dry and packed. He had brought nothing to dig with. He knelt and used his hands, and then a flat stone, and the light went from gold to gray while he worked. His fingernails split. He kept going.

The tin was deeper than he remembered. When the stone struck metal, he stopped breathing for a moment, then clawed the rest of the dirt away and lifted it out. The roses had faded almost to nothing. The lid was rusted shut.

He sat back on his heels and held it in his lap. Behind him, footsteps came through the grass, slow and even, the way she had always walked when she wanted him to turn around first.

Hollis did not turn. He closed his eyes and waited, and the footsteps stopped just close enough.

## Hop 10

The lighthouse had been automated the spring Tomas turned twelve, and his grandfather, who had kept it lit for thirty-one years, had been given a pension and a framed letter of thanks signed by someone in the capital. The letter hung in the hallway. His grandfather walked past it without looking.

Tomas still climbed the spiral stairs every evening at dusk. No one asked him to. The lamp switched itself on now by a sensor that measured the failing light more precisely than any human eye, and the logbook where his grandfather had once recorded weather, ships, and the occasional whale had been replaced by a data feed monitored from a harbor office ninety miles away, where no one had ever tasted salt on the wind. But his grandfather had taught him the names of the boats before the men in suits arrived, and the habit of watching the water had outlasted the job that had once demanded it.

From the gallery he could see the fishing fleet come home, the hulls turning copper in the last sun, the gulls following like a loose thread unspooling behind them.

One evening in early March a small dinghy sat too far out, its single sail luffing, going nowhere. A girl stood in it, hauling at a line that would not answer.

Tomas did not shout. He lifted the old brass horn from its hook and gave two short blasts, then one long, the way his grandfather had shown him.

She looked up. He pointed toward the channel, where the current ran easy. She turned the bow, caught the wind, and came in.

When she reached the rocks she called, "How did you know?"

Tomas felt his grandfather's stillness settle over him, and understood that the light had never really been the lamp.

## Hop 20

The lighthouse at Corrie Head had been automated in the year Ivo Marchant turned nineteen, and for a while nobody noticed that he kept going up anyway.

His grandfather had been the last keeper. The old man had taught Ivo to read the lamp the way other people read weather: the faint stutter in the rotation that meant salt on the bearings, the particular hum of the generator when the fuel was running thin, the way the beam looked thicker on the water when a sea fog was building out past the Sisters.

The new system reported all of this to a control room on the mainland, ninety miles south. It reported it well. Ivo did not distrust the machine so much as he distrusted the idea that a light could be watched from somewhere it could not be seen.

So he climbed the hundred and twelve steps most evenings and sat with a thermos in the lamp room, saying nothing to anyone about it.

In February a cable fault took the telemetry down. The control room showed the light as operating normally, because that was the last thing it had heard. The lamp, in fact, had stopped turning at half past nine.

Ivo saw the fixed beam from the harbour wall and was up the steps in twelve minutes. He cranked the mechanism by hand, as his grandfather had shown him once, half as a joke, and kept it turning until the relief crew arrived at dawn.

The fault report noted a manual restart of unknown origin.

Ivo bought a new thermos.

## Hop 30

The measuring took most of the afternoon. She used his brass dividers, the ones with the worn thumb-grip, and walked them across the paper the way she had watched him do a hundred times without ever asking why. Forty-one miles, near enough. Bearing a little north of east. She wrote both figures on the first clean page of the notebook and then sat looking at them as though they might rearrange themselves into a sentence.

At half past ten she switched the set on. The hum came up, then the hiss, then nothing. She waited. The clock on the shelf was loud in the empty room. At eleven minutes past she picked up the pencil, held it over the page, and put it down again. There was nothing to write. Whatever he had heard, it had not come for her.

She tried the next night, and the next. On the fourth night she fell asleep in the chair and woke with the notebook on the floor and the set still hissing and a feeling like she had missed a train.

So she stopped listening and started reading instead. She went back to the beginning, the first entry, the ink gone brown. A number. A time. A single word she had always assumed was a call sign. She said it aloud in the kitchen and it did not sound like a call sign. It sounded like a name.

In the morning she went down to the harbour again and this time she stepped onto the boat.

## Hop 40

The cold went through her wrists like water through a net. She had expected it to burn. Instead it simply took, patiently, the way the tide takes a footprint, and she felt the chart's coastlines settle into her the same way she had once felt a new harbor settle into memory: not learned, but arrived at.

He was sitting. She had not seen him do it. His hands lay open on his knees, and the pale mark on his palm was already fading to ordinary skin.

"You should have argued longer," he said.

"You should have stood less." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Go on. The causeway will hold until the light turns."

He did not go. He looked at the boards between them, at the salt crust, at the ring under her fingers. "The first night is the worst. The sea comes in to see what you are. It leans hard. You will want to let go."

"Did you?"

"Every night." He stood, slowly, testing the floor as if it might not be there. "That is why it works. Someone has to want to and not."

At the door he paused, and she thought he would say her name, though she had never given it. He said instead, "The reef. You were wrong by two cables, not one."

Then the door, and the dark, and far out past the edge of her lantern, the sound of water beginning to remember itself, coming back across the sand toward the only shore it had.

## Hop 50

She waited. He gave her that; she did not fidget or look at her watch or glance back at the car the way most people would have. She simply stood at the gate with the envelope held against her coat and let the wind do what it liked with her hair.

He drank the tea. It was stone cold and he drank all of it, because he had said he would, or because Tom had, and it came to the same thing now. When the cup was empty he set it on the step beside him and stood, slower than he would have liked, one hand on the doorframe.

"You'd better come in," he said. "There's more in the pot."

She came through the gate and up the path, and he saw her take in the yard as she passed: the woodpile under its tarp, the tractor that had not moved in two years, the dog's bowl by the water butt with nothing in it and no dog. She did not remark on any of it.

Inside, the kitchen was warm from the range. He put the kettle on the hob out of habit and stood with his back to her while it began to tick.

"How did he go?"

"In his sleep. Or near enough." She set the envelope on the table, squarely, in the middle. "He was talking about you at the end. Not to me. To the room."

Aldous turned. The envelope sat there with his name on it in that slanting hand, the A too large, the way Tom had always written it.

"What did he say?"

"That you'd argue with the letter before you opened it." She pulled out a chair. "He said to give you the time."

## Hop 56

The orchard had gone to scrub years before she came back, and she found him out there anyway, standing between two rows with a pruning saw he had no business using at his age. The dog lay in the tall grass watching him, the way it watched everything now, without getting up.

"Those trees are dead," she said.

"Some of them."

He set the saw against his knee and looked at the branch he had been working on, gray and brittle, the bark peeling in long strips. Then he looked at her, and she saw him decide not to say the thing he had been going to say.

"Your mother planted this row," he said instead.

"I know which row she planted."

She walked past him to the end where the fence had fallen and stood there with her hands in her coat. Beyond it the field ran down to the creek, brown and flat, and a heron stood in the shallows not moving, and she watched it not move for a long time.

When she came back he had cut the branch and it lay in the grass between them like something neither of them wanted to claim.

"I'm not selling," he said.

"I didn't ask you to."

"You were going to."

She picked up the branch and carried it to the burn pile without answering. The dog got up then, slow, and followed her, and behind them she heard the saw start again on the next limb, steady, unhurried, the sound of a man with more work than daylight and no intention of stopping for either.

## Hop 57

The kitchen still had the calendar from three years ago nailed by the door, turned to a month of someone else's birthdays. She did not take it down. She made coffee in the percolator because the electric one had died and he had never replaced it, and she stood at the window while it worked itself up to a boil.

He came in stamping mud off his boots and did not take them off.

"You could have called first," he said.

"I did call. Twice."

He went to the sink and ran water over his hands, which were shaking a little, and she watched him notice her watching and turn his back so she couldn't.

"Phone's in the barn."

"The phone's not in the barn."

"It was for a while."

She poured two cups and put one on the table in front of his chair, the one with the arm mended in wire, and sat down across from it. He stayed at the sink a while longer than the washing required.

"Doctor in town says you missed August," she said.

"Doctor in town says a lot of things."

"He called me, Dad."

That stopped him. He dried his hands slowly on the towel that had been hanging there since she was a girl, or one just like it, and then he came and sat, and lifted the cup, and set it down again without drinking.

"He had no business."

"No," she said. "He didn't."

Outside the dog barked once at nothing and gave up. The coffee steamed between them. Neither reached for it, and neither got up, and the calendar by the door went on saying it was a year that had already happened.

## Hop 58

The porch light had been burned out so long the moths had stopped coming. She found the spare key where it had always been, under the third flowerpot, though nothing had grown in the pot for years and the soil had gone to gray dust.

Inside, the television was on with the sound off. He was asleep in the recliner with a newspaper across his chest, and she stood in the doorway a minute deciding whether to wake him or let the house do it.

The house did it. A board gave under her heel and his eyes came open.

"You're early," he said.

"I'm two days late."

He folded the paper with more care than it deserved and set it on the table next to a plate with the remains of something, toast maybe, from that morning or another morning.

"Traffic?"

"There's no traffic between here and anywhere, Dad."

She went to the kitchen without asking and opened the refrigerator. Milk, a jar of pickles, half an onion in wax paper. She closed it again and came back and sat on the arm of the couch, the way she had when she was twelve and he'd told her not to.

"Marlene at the pharmacy says you haven't picked up since June."

"Marlene talks."

"Marlene's right, though."

He looked at the television, where a man was pointing at a map of weather that had already come and gone. His hand went to the remote and stopped short of it.

"I've been meaning to."

"I know."

The man on the screen kept pointing. Neither of them turned up the sound.

## Hop 59

The laundromat on Fourth stayed open all night, though the sign had said CLOSED for as long as anyone could remember. Ruth came in a little after two with a pillowcase of towels and found the old man already there, sitting in the plastic chair by the dryers with nothing tumbling behind him.

"Machine three eats quarters," he said, not looking up.

"I know. I use five."

She fed the coins in and listened to the water start. The fluorescent tube over the folding table flickered the way it always had, and someone had left a single sock, child-sized, on the sill where the window used to open.

He had a paper cup of coffee that had stopped steaming some time ago. She sat two chairs down and put her feet up on the bench.

"Couldn't sleep?" she asked.

"Didn't try."

The washer thumped through its cycle. Outside a truck went by slow, headlights sweeping the wall of dryers and moving on.

"My daughter's coming Thursday," he said.

"That's good."

"She'll want to see the house."

Ruth thought about the house, which she had seen, and about the yard, which she had also seen. She picked at a thread on the pillowcase.

"You've got till Thursday."

"I've had till Thursday before."

The machine kicked into spin and the whole row shuddered. He finished the cold coffee anyway and set the cup down on the floor beside his shoe, careful, like it mattered where it went.

When her towels were done she folded them slowly. He was still there when she left. She didn't say goodnight and he didn't seem to expect it.

## Hop 60

The bus shelter on Ninth had lost its glass years ago, so it was just a roof on two poles and a bench nobody had bothered to remove. Della got there at quarter past five with her thermos and found the boy already sitting at the far end, backpack between his knees, staring at the street.

"The 22 doesn't run this early," she said.

"I know."

She sat at the other end and unscrewed the thermos. Steam came off it and vanished. Across the road the bakery had its lights on but the door was still locked, and a man inside moved trays around without looking out.

"You waiting for somebody?" she asked.

"Not really."

A streetlight down the block buzzed and went dark, then came back weaker. The boy zipped his jacket to the chin. He couldn't have been more than sixteen.

"My mom works nights," he said after a while. "She gets home around six."

"So you wait here."

"Sometimes."

Della poured a little coffee into the lid and held it out. He looked at it, then took it, and drank without saying thank you, which she didn't mind.

"She know you're out here?"

"She knows I'm not asleep."

The first bus of the day rounded the corner two blocks up, empty, its interior lit like a fish tank. It slowed for the shelter out of habit, saw nobody stand, and went on.

At six a woman in scrubs came around the corner walking fast. The boy handed the lid back and picked up his bag. He didn't wave and Della didn't expect it. She screwed the thermos shut and waited for the 22.
