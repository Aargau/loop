## Hop 1

Mara found the brass key beneath a loose floorboard in the room her grandfather had used as a workshop. It was smaller than her thumb, dark with age, and tied to a strip of blue ribbon. She had spent the morning sorting jars of screws and polishing tools, expecting nothing more mysterious than dust. The key changed her plans.

She searched the workshop for a lock it might fit. The cabinets were open, the drawers empty, and the door to the garden stood wide. At last, she noticed a narrow wooden panel behind the workbench. Its grain formed a crooked spiral, almost hidden beneath layers of varnish. When she pressed the center, the panel clicked inward.

Behind it was a shallow compartment containing a folded map and a packet of letters. The map showed the village as it had been decades earlier, before the railway crossed the river. A red line led from the workshop to a small island marked only with a star. On the first letter, her grandfather had written her name.

Mara sat on the floor and read. He described a place where he and her grandmother had gone whenever the world felt too loud: a willow-covered island with a spring that ran clear even in the driest summer. He had meant to take Mara there, but time had slipped away. The key, he wrote, opened the gate at the old footbridge.

By afternoon, Mara had packed the map and a bottle of water. She locked the workshop behind her and followed the river path, wondering what else her grandfather had left for her to discover.

## Hop 2

When the rain stopped, the town’s clock began running backward. At first, nobody noticed. Shopkeepers swept water from their doorways, children jumped over puddles, and the baker set fresh loaves in the window. Only the clockmaker, Ivo, saw its hands turning against the sun.

He climbed the tower stairs with a lantern and a set of tiny tools. Behind the clock face, he found a blue moth beating its wings against the gears. Its scales shimmered like the evening sky. Ivo reached toward it, but the moth slipped through a crack in the stone and vanished.

The hands stopped. Below, the town went quiet. A woman at the fountain stared at her empty bucket. The baker’s oven cooled, and a boy’s half-built paper boat sagged in the gutter. For one strange moment, everyone seemed to be waiting for something they had forgotten.

Ivo searched the tower until he found a narrow stair leading to a room beneath the clock. There, the blue moth rested on a wooden box. Inside lay a bundle of old photographs, each showing the town on the day its clock had first been built. In every picture, someone stood alone at the edge of the crowd.

The clockmaker understood. The moth had been feeding on memories left unshared. He carried the photographs to the square and began asking people who the strangers were. Stories followed: a lost sister, a brave teacher, a friend who had moved away.

As the townspeople spoke, the moth rose into the air. It circled the clock once, then flew toward the river. The hands started forward again. This time, everyone watched.

## Hop 3

On the first morning of winter, Mara found a door in the snow. It stood upright beside the old train tracks, painted green and fitted with a brass handle. There was no wall around it, only drifts of white stretching toward the pine forest.

She tugged the handle. Warm air breathed through the opening, carrying the smell of oranges and woodsmoke. Beyond the threshold lay a crowded station, bright with lamps. Travelers hurried across the platform, though every clock showed the same time: seven minutes past midnight.

A conductor in a red scarf approached. “You’re late,” he said, handing her a ticket with no destination printed on it. “The train leaves when you remember where you’re going.”

Mara thought of home: the narrow kitchen, her father’s silent chair, the suitcase she had packed after their argument. She had meant to leave before dawn, but the snow had covered the road. Now she stood with the ticket trembling in her glove.

A whistle sounded somewhere underground. The conductor waited. Mara could step aboard and let the train carry her away, or turn back through the green door. Neither choice felt easy.

Then, from the station’s far end, came a familiar tune—her father humming the song he used to sing while fixing the radio. Mara looked through the open doorway. On the snowy track, a lantern bobbed closer, and a figure called her name.

The ticket warmed in her hand. Its blank face filled with a single word: “Home.” Mara folded it carefully and stepped back into the winter night. Behind her, the train departed without a sound.

## Hop 10

Every morning, before the bakery opened, Lila placed a small loaf on the windowsill. She never left a note or watched to see who took it. By noon, the loaf was always gone.

One frosty Tuesday, a boy in a red scarf paused outside. He looked at the bread, then at the dark shop behind it. After a moment, he tucked the loaf under his arm and hurried away.

The next morning, Lila left another. This time she added a paper bag of apples. The boy came again, but he didn’t take them. Instead, he pushed a folded note beneath the bag.

“Thank you,” it read. “My little sister is sick. We haven’t had much to eat.”

Lila read the note twice. Then she filled a basket with bread, fruit, and a jar of honey. She carried it to the narrow blue house at the end of the lane.

The boy opened the door. Behind him, a little girl sat wrapped in blankets, her cheeks flushed but her eyes bright.

“I’m Lila,” she said. “I made too much.”

The boy studied her face, uncertain. “You don’t have to do this.”

“I know,” Lila replied. “That’s why it matters.”

After that, the basket appeared on the bakery windowsill every morning. Some days it held rolls; some days, soup in a warm jar. Neighbors began adding things of their own: a scarf, a book, a bunch of flowers.

By spring, the blue house was lively again. The little girl was well, and her brother helped Lila carry baskets through the neighborhood. The windowsill stayed empty for a while—but no one forgot what had begun there.

## Hop 20

Every autumn, the people of Bellweather gathered beside the river to watch the lantern boats drift beneath the old stone bridge. The tradition had begun generations ago, when fishermen carried lights on the water to guide one another home through the fog. This year, however, the river was low, and the boats kept catching on the muddy bank.

Mara had come with her grandfather, who had built a small blue boat from scraps of cedar. He had painted a bright yellow star on its sail, though his hands trembled too much to tie the final knot. Mara offered to finish it, but he shook his head.

“Let’s see what the river teaches us first,” he said.

As twilight settled, the crowd placed their boats in the water. Most drifted a few yards before stopping. Mara’s blue boat spun in a slow circle, its paper lantern flickering. She was about to reach for it when she noticed a narrow current flowing close to the far bank, hidden beneath willow branches.

She and her grandfather carried the boat upstream and set it down near the gentle stream. This time, it glided forward, turning toward the bridge. Soon other families followed, guiding their lanterns into the same current. One by one, the lights slipped beneath the stones and reappeared on the other side.

The crowd cheered. Her grandfather leaned on Mara’s shoulder, smiling as the blue boat sailed onward. Its small light shone against the dark water, carrying the old tradition into another year—and reminding everyone that sometimes a new path is already there, waiting to be noticed.

## Hop 30

Every Thursday, Mara walked to the harbor before sunrise to unlock the little blue shed where her grandfather had repaired fishing nets. He had been gone for three winters, but she still kept his tools arranged on the workbench, each needle and spool in its proper place.

One morning, she found a folded note tucked inside a cracked lantern. The paper smelled faintly of salt. “For whoever opens this,” it began. “The sea returns what it can, but not always in the shape we expect.” Beneath the words was a rough drawing of a gull with one crooked wing.

Mara recognized the bird. For weeks, a young gull had been hopping around the pier, unable to fly. She had left scraps of fish nearby, though it always waited until she turned away to eat. That day, she carried the lantern outside and set it on the stones. The gull pecked at its glass, then shuffled closer.

At the harbor’s edge, an old fisherman called out that the tide was turning. Mara looked down. A strip of net had caught around the bird’s leg. She crouched, spoke softly, and worked the knots loose with her grandfather’s smallest needle. The gull flapped once, stumbled, then lifted into the wind.

It circled the shed before vanishing over the breakwater.

Mara returned the note to the lantern, but she added a line beneath her grandfather’s: “Some things come back when we make room for them.” Then she opened the shed doors wide, letting the morning light fall across the tools.

## Hop 40

The stories spread farther than Mara expected. Within a week, people came to the harbor carrying photographs, letters, and scraps of memory. Some remembered the families from the list; others recognized names in the faded handwriting. The dockmaster pinned every discovery to the wall of the old boathouse, turning it into a patchwork of faces and dates.

One afternoon, a woman named Elian arrived with a tin of seashells. Her grandmother, she said, had collected them as a child on the northern shore. Beneath the shells lay a small brass plate engraved with the same coastline as the map on Mara’s box. A second silver pin marked the clock tower.

Mara hurried to the tower with Elian and the dockmaster. Its door had been locked for decades, but the brass key fitted the narrow lock. Inside, dust coated the stairs. At the top, they found a wooden drawer built into the wall. It held a bundle of letters, each addressed to someone named on the list.

The letters described a storm that had forced the families to leave the coast. They had promised to return, but years passed, and the promise faded into family legend. Mara read the final letter aloud. It ended with an invitation: “When the harbor remembers us, let us meet again by the water.”

That evening, the townspeople lit lanterns along the dock. Elian brought her grandchildren, and the dockmaster rang the tower bell. Mara watched the reflections tremble across the waves. The harbor, she realized, had not simply kept the past safe. It had been waiting for someone to open the door.

## Hop 50

The island rose from the dark like the back of a sleeping whale. Peter guided the boat toward a narrow strip of shore, where the waves broke softly over black stones. The golden flicker vanished.

For a moment, no one moved.

Then Ada lifted the lantern. “We brought our own light,” she said, though her voice trembled.

They stepped onto the island and followed a path almost swallowed by grass. The keeper’s hut stood beyond a low ridge, its roof bent beneath years of wind. A thin ribbon of smoke curled from the chimney.

Peter knocked. The door opened at once.

An old woman stood in the doorway, holding a small oil lamp. Her silver hair was braided over one shoulder. She looked first at Peter, then at the compass in his hand.

“You took your time,” she said.

He knew her voice, though he had not heard it since he was a boy. “Elian?”

She smiled. “Tomas said you might come when you were ready.”

Inside, the hut smelled of ash and salt. On the table lay a second map, marked with the same blue line. Beside it rested a letter, its paper browned at the edges.

Peter recognized Tomas’s handwriting.

“Why did he send us here?” Mara asked.

Elian passed the letter to Peter. “Because the light was never meant to guide him home,” she said. “It was meant to show you where to begin.”

Outside, the lamp shone through the window, bright enough to reach the far shore. Peter unfolded the letter, and the first words made his breath catch: THERE IS ANOTHER ISLAND.

## Hop 56

Peter snatched up the compass. Its needle spun wildly, then pointed inland, toward the dark line of trees above the cove.

Ada waited at the edge of the stones, one hand pressed to her side. “Did it take anything from you?”

Peter tried to answer, but the words caught in his throat. He could still hear his mother’s lullaby—not the creature’s flawless version, but the crooked one he had sung. The one with the missing line and the ridiculous ending.

“I don’t think so,” he said.

Elian leaned over the compass. A thin crack ran across its glass, though the needle held steady. “It’s pointing away from the water.”

“Then we follow it,” Ada said. Her gaze lingered on the empty cove. “Before the tide comes back.”

They climbed the path in silence. Behind them, the sea slid over the stones, covering the place where the bells had rested. No note followed. No pale light stirred beneath the waves.

At the top of the path, Peter looked back once. The cove seemed ordinary now: a crescent of dark water beneath a sky bruised with clouds. Still, he thought he saw something white moving far below the surface.

He tightened his grip on the compass and faced the trees.

A narrow trail wound between the trunks. At its entrance stood a weather-beaten post, its lettering nearly worn away. Elian brushed off the moss.

“Northwatch,” she read. “That’s the name on the map.”

The compass needle trembled, then pointed down the trail.

From somewhere in the forest came a soft, familiar tune. Peter stopped.

Ada took his arm. “Keep walking,” she whispered.

This time, the song did not sound like his mother.

## Hop 57

Peter kept walking, though every step seemed to carry the tune deeper into his head. It drifted between the trees, gentle and low, like someone humming to keep themselves company. The melody was almost his mother’s, but it turned at the wrong places, twisting into notes she had never sung.

“Don’t listen,” Ada said.

“I’m trying.”

Elian unfolded the map as they walked. Its ink had blurred where the cove was marked, leaving a gray smear across the coastline. Northwatch, however, remained clear. A small black tower stood at the end of a thin trail, and beside it someone had drawn a circle around a single word: *Remember*.

“Do you know what that means?” Peter asked.

Elian shook her head. “Not yet.”

The trail climbed steadily. Wet branches brushed their shoulders, and the forest floor grew soft beneath their boots. Behind them, the tune followed, never louder, never farther away.

At last, the trees opened onto a ridge. Beyond it stood Northwatch: a squat stone tower with a roof that had partly collapsed. Its windows were dark. A rusted bell hung above the doorway, motionless in the wind.

Ada let go of Peter’s arm. “That bell wasn’t on the map.”

The compass needle jerked toward it. A hairline crack spread across the glass.

Peter stepped closer. The song behind them stopped.

For one quiet moment, the forest held its breath. Then the bell rang once, though no hand had touched it.

Peter felt the sound pass through him—not as a note, but as a question.

From inside the tower, someone answered in his mother’s voice.

## Hop 58

Peter froze at the doorway. The voice had come from the darkness beyond it, soft and unmistakable.

“Peter?”

Ada caught his sleeve. “That isn’t her.”

The bell swayed above them, though the wind had fallen still. Its rusted clapper tapped the rim again, making no sound. Elian raised the map. The inked circle around *Remember* had begun to bleed outward, spreading in thin black branches.

Peter reached for the door.

“Wait,” Elian said. “What do you remember?”

He turned toward her. The question seemed simple, but the answer slipped away. His mother’s face, the color of her coat, the sound of her laugh—all drifted behind the melody in his mind.

Then he remembered a kitchen window streaked with rain. His mother standing beside it, one hand resting on his hair. She had been humming a tune, but not the one that had followed them through the woods.

“She sang when she was afraid,” he said. “She told me the song was a lantern. You carried it until you found your way home.”

The darkness inside the tower shifted.

“Peter,” the voice called again. This time it sounded strained, almost frightened.

Ada tightened her grip. “A lantern can lead you somewhere you don’t want to go.”

Peter looked at the cracked compass. Its needle pointed not at the tower, but at his own chest.

He stepped over the threshold. The air inside smelled of cold stone and smoke. On the far wall, a narrow staircase spiraled downward, though Northwatch had no basement on the map.

From below came a faint, familiar hum.

Peter began to descend.

## Hop 59

Each step downward dimmed the world above. Ada followed close behind, one hand on the wall, while Elian brought up the rear with the map held like a shield. The humming grew clearer, then stopped.

At the foot of the stairs stood a room no larger than a pantry. A lantern rested on a stone table. Its glass was dark, but a small flame burned inside, blue as winter twilight.

Beside it lay a wooden music box. Peter recognized the carving on its lid: a crooked little bird his mother had once drawn on the margins of his schoolbooks.

He reached for it.

“Don’t,” Ada whispered.

The lantern flared. On the far wall, a shadow appeared—a woman’s silhouette, one hand raised as if beckoning. Peter’s throat tightened.

“Peter, come home.”

The voice was his mother’s, but now he heard the wrongness in it. She had never called him home in that frightened, pleading tone. She had always made home sound like a promise, not a warning.

He took the lantern instead.

The blue flame brightened, revealing words etched into the table: *A memory given freely cannot be stolen.*

Elian leaned closer to read them. “It wants something from you.”

Peter thought of the kitchen window, the rain, his mother’s hand resting gently on his hair. He held that memory in his mind—not the melody, not the shadow, but the warmth of her palm.

Then he spoke the tune she had truly sung, a simple lullaby with no strange notes in it.

The music box snapped shut. The shadow folded inward, vanishing like a candle pinched between fingers.

Somewhere above, the bell rang once.

This time, they heard it.

## Hop 60

The bell’s note trembled through the stone, then faded. For a moment, none of them moved.

A narrow seam appeared in the far wall. Blue light spilled through it, outlining a door that had not been there before. Peter lifted the lantern. Its flame leaned toward the opening, though no breeze stirred.

“Is that the way out?” Elian asked.

“Maybe,” Ada said. “Or maybe it wants us to think so.”

Peter looked down at the music box. The carved bird seemed almost alive in the wavering light, its beak tilted toward the hidden door. He remembered his mother’s hands guiding his over the tiny brass key, teaching him how to wind the box without breaking its spring.

A second bell rang, louder than the first.

The door swung inward.

Beyond it lay a hallway lined with mirrors. In each one, Peter saw the three of them from a different angle: Ada with a crown of thorny branches, Elian carrying an empty map, himself alone beneath a dark sky. Their reflections did not move when they did.

“Keep your eyes on the floor,” Ada warned.

They stepped through. The lantern’s glow dwindled, leaving only a thin blue path across the stones. Behind them, the door began to close.

Peter heard his mother’s lullaby again—not from the music box, but from somewhere ahead. This time the melody was broken, each note separated by a long, uncertain silence.

He stopped.

“That isn’t the tune I sang,” he said.

“No,” Ada replied, gripping his sleeve. “So don’t answer it.”

Together, they followed the blue path deeper into the hall.
