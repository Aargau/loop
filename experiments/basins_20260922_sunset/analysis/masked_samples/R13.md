## Hop 1

The lighthouse keeper climbed the spiral staircase for the last time that season, counting each iron step as he had done for nearly thirty years. One hundred and twelve. The number never changed, but his knees told him it grew larger every winter. At the top, the great lens sat silent beneath its canvas cover, waiting for the automated system that would replace him in the spring. He pulled the cover away and ran his palm along the cool glass prisms, remembering the storms they had cut through, the fishing boats they had guided home, and the single night a freighter had nearly struck the rocks before turning at the final moment. Outside, the sea was calm and gray, stretching toward a horizon softened by mist. Gulls circled below, calling to one another in voices that sounded almost like laughter. He had kept a logbook every day of his service, and the shelves downstairs now held forty-three volumes filled with weather reports, ship sightings, and small observations no one would ever read: the morning a whale surfaced near the jetty, the afternoon his daughter visited with her newborn son, the evening the power failed and he turned the lens by hand until dawn. He opened the final volume and wrote the date carefully. Beneath it, after a long pause, he added a single line: The light will go on without me, as it should. Then he closed the book, replaced the cover over the lens, and began the long walk down, counting each step again.

## Hop 2

The baker unlocked the shop at four in the morning, as she had every day since her grandmother handed her the keys. The bell above the door gave its familiar tired jingle, and the smell of yesterday's bread still hung in the air like a memory refusing to leave. She tied on her apron, switched on the ovens, and listened to them tick as they warmed. Flour dusted the wooden counter where three generations of hands had kneaded dough, and a shallow groove had formed near the center, worn smooth by decades of patient pressing. She measured water, salt, and yeast without looking at the scale, trusting the weight of the bowl in her arms. Outside, the street was dark and empty except for a delivery truck rumbling past and a cat watching from the windowsill across the road. By five, the first loaves were rising beneath linen towels, swelling slowly like sleeping animals breathing. She thought about the letter on the kitchen table upstairs, the one from the developer offering more money than the shop had earned in twenty years. She had read it four times and still had not answered. When the timer sounded, she pulled a tray of golden rolls from the oven and set them on the rack to cool. The crust crackled softly as it met the cooler air, a sound her grandmother used to call the bread singing. She stood still and listened until it faded. Then she turned the sign on the door to open, poured herself a cup of coffee, and waited for the neighborhood to wake.

## Hop 3

The lighthouse keeper climbed the spiral stairs at dusk, counting each of the one hundred and twelve steps the way his father had taught him. The iron railing was cold beneath his palm, and the wind pressed against the tower walls with a low, steady moan. At the top, he wiped salt from the thick glass and checked the brass fittings, polishing a smudge that no ship would ever notice. The lamp had been automated nine years ago, and a technician from the mainland visited twice a year to make sure the sensors still worked. No one needed him here anymore. Still, he came every evening, because the light seemed lonelier without someone to watch it turn. Below, the sea churned gray and restless, throwing white spray against the rocks where a fishing boat had broken apart long before he was born. He kept a small notebook in his coat pocket and wrote down the weather each night: wind direction, cloud cover, the color of the water. The pages were filling faster now, and he wondered who would read them when he was gone. His daughter called on Sundays and asked when he would move to the city to live with her family. He always said soon. Tonight the clouds broke apart just as the lamp began to sweep, and a thin slice of moon appeared above the horizon. He watched the beam pass over the waves, again and again, steady as a heartbeat. Then he sat on the narrow bench, opened his notebook, and wrote a single word: clear.

## Hop 10

The baker unlocked her shop at four each morning, when the street outside was still dark and silent except for the hum of the streetlamps. She tied on her flour-stained apron, lit the old brick oven, and set out the dough that had been rising overnight in wooden trays. Her grandmother had built the business sixty years earlier with a single oven and a recipe for rye bread scribbled on the back of an envelope. That envelope now hung framed above the counter, faded to the color of weak tea. The neighborhood had changed around the little shop. A supermarket had opened two blocks away, selling sliced loaves in plastic bags for half her price, and a developer had twice offered to buy the building. She had listened politely and sent him away with a warm cinnamon roll. By six o'clock the first customers arrived: a nurse coming off the night shift, a pair of construction workers, an elderly man who bought one poppy-seed roll every day and paid in exact change. She knew their names, their children's names, and how they liked their bread. In a spiral notebook beside the register she kept track of special orders and small kindnesses: the birthday cake for a boy who had been sick all spring, the loaves she left each Friday at the shelter on Maple Street, the winter the power failed and she baked by lantern light. When the shelves were empty by early afternoon, she swept the floor, counted the till, and walked home tired, flour still in her hair, but content.

## Hop 20

February brought a thaw that lasted only three days, just long enough to turn the street to slush before the cold returned and locked it all into ridges of dirty ice. It was during that brief softening that Mira first heard the sound. She was sorting invoices at the front counter when it drifted from the back room, a faint, uneven ticking, halting and stubborn, like a heart learning its rhythm. She set down her pen and listened. It stopped, started again, faltered, and then, for nearly a full minute, held steady before collapsing into silence. That evening Tobias said nothing about it, and she did not ask. But she saw that his hands were nicked in new places and that the notebook had grown thick with crossed-out pages, whole sections scored through with impatient lines. Once she caught him staring at the old regulator clock on the wall, the one her master had built, as if measuring himself against it. The following week he stayed home with a fever, and in his absence she allowed herself to lift the cloth covering his bench. Beneath it sat a small, ungainly mechanism, its plates filed by hand and not quite square, its wheels crowded together like passengers in a narrow carriage. It was ugly. It was also, she realized with a quiet jolt, doing something she had never seen a movement do. She replaced the cloth exactly as she had found it, brushed a stray shaving from the edge of the bench, and went back to the counter, her pulse ticking unevenly in her ears.

## Hop 30

Mira snatched her hand away as if the page had bitten her. The pencil rolled from her fingers and clicked against the floorboards, yet the scratching went on, steady and unhurried, like someone writing a letter they had composed many times before. Tobias struck the match himself. The flare was small and orange, and in its trembling circle they bent over the notebook together. Beneath her clumsy capitals, in a slanting hand the color of old rust, new words were forming: YOU DID MEAN IT. YOU WROTE MY NAME. Mira's throat went dry. She had written no name. She had copied only what her grandmother left on the first page, a line of strange syllables she had taken for a nursery rhyme. The match burned down to Tobias's fingertips, and he dropped it with a hiss. In the dark, the scratching paused, then resumed. "What's it saying?" he whispered. She did not answer. She was counting the knocks that had come before, three, then three again, and remembering how her grandmother always rapped on the kitchen table three times before she said grace. On the porch the boards fell quiet, as though whatever waited there was listening too. Mira found the pencil by feel, pressed the notebook open, and wrote beneath the rusty letters, her hand shaking so badly the words would barely hold together: WHO ARE YOU. The paper warmed again. This time the answer came quickly, only four letters, and Mira knew them before the last stroke was finished. They spelled the name she had called her grandmother as a child.

## Hop 40

By morning the storm had passed, leaving the sky scrubbed clean and the roads glittering with puddles. Mira spread the letters across the kitchen table while Tobias brewed coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. She had not slept. Every time she closed her eyes, she heard that voice again, faint and patient beneath the rush of the tide. "Read me the first one," Tobias said, setting a mug beside her. Mira slid her thumb beneath the cracked wax. The paper inside was thin as onion skin, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. "My dearest Elias," she read aloud. "The light was dark again last night. I watched from the cliff until the cold drove me home. They say you have gone to the mainland, but I do not believe them. You would never leave the lamp untended." Tobias frowned. "The date?" "June 1952." She sifted through the others, lining them up by postmark that had never been stamped, only written in her grandmother's careful hand. The last was dated nearly a year later. Its envelope was thicker than the rest, and something shifted inside when she tilted it. Mira hesitated. Her grandmother had kept these secrets for seventy years, hidden in a crate beneath the eaves. Opening them felt like trespassing. "We could leave it," Tobias said gently. Mira looked at the brass key lying beside the shell. Harrow Point was only three hours north. "No," she said, tucking the letter into her coat. "We're going to find out what happened to him."

## Hop 50

They stepped out onto the gallery together. The air smelled of salt and something older, like cedar chests opened after many years. Below them the rocks were smooth and dry, and the waves had folded themselves into long, silver ribbons that did not break. Mira looked back once. Through the glass she could see the lantern room exactly as they had left it: the logbook open on the desk, the pen resting in its crease, the kettle on the little stove that had never, she understood now, been warm. The beam swept past her and through her, and she felt nothing but a faint, pleasant brightness, like sun on closed eyelids. "Who will keep it now?" Tobias asked. The man from the photograph smiled and pointed down the coast. Far off, where the headland curved away into darkness, a new light was blinking to life, steady and white, electric and unmanned. It did not need trimming. It did not need anyone to climb the steps. "Ships will see it," the man said. "They always find their way home." He started down the stairs, and his footsteps made no sound on the iron. Tobias followed, then paused and held out his hand to her, the way he had on their wedding day, when the chapel had been cold and he had been so nervous he dropped the ring. Mira laughed, a sound she had almost forgotten. She took his hand. Behind them, the old lamp flickered once, gently, as if in farewell, and then the tower stood dark and quiet against a sky full of stars.

## Hop 56

The next morning Mira woke to gulls and the soft knock of hulls against the pilings. Tobias was already gone, his boots missing from beside the door, the kettle still faintly warm. She found him where she expected, at the top of the old lighthouse, polishing a lens that no longer needed polishing. "Habit," he said, before she could tease him. From up there the whole village lay open like a hand: the church with its crooked weathervane, the schoolhouse, the green where the fair used to be. Smoke curled from chimneys that had been cold for years. Someone was hanging washing on Gull Row, and the sheets snapped white and bright. "That's the Abernethy girl," Tobias said, squinting. "The one who drowned in the storm of '52." Mira looked, and the girl was a grown woman now, singing as she pinned. Down at the quay the young man from yesterday was helping mend nets, sleeves rolled, laughing at something the harbormaster said. He looked up once toward the lighthouse and raised a hand. Tobias raised his in return. "He asked me how long he'd be staying," Tobias said. "What did you tell him?" "That nobody's in a hurry here." Mira leaned against the rail. The sea stretched out silver and endless, and far on the horizon another sail was coming, small as a moth. Tobias set down his cloth. "I'll just go and see," he said, and she let him, because she knew he would always go, and because he would always come back.

## Hop 57

By noon the sail had become a boat, and the boat had become a small blue dinghy with a patched canvas and a single figure at the tiller. Mira watched from the lighthouse as Tobias waited at the end of the quay, hands in his pockets, rocking on his heels the way he did when he was pretending not to be nervous. The dinghy bumped against the stones. An old woman climbed out, slow and careful, holding a wicker basket against her chest. She wore a yellow headscarf faded almost to cream. Tobias took the basket from her, and for a long moment neither of them moved. Then the woman reached up and touched his face, and Mira understood. She had seen the photograph a hundred times, propped on the mantel beside the clock: a young woman in that same scarf, laughing into the wind. His mother. Mira went down the spiral stairs quickly, her hand skimming the cold iron rail. By the time she reached the quay the two of them were sitting on an upturned crate, sharing bread from the basket, and Tobias was crying without seeming to notice. "You must be Mira," the woman said, and made room for her. "He wrote to me about you. Every letter." "He never sent any letters," Mira said gently. "No," the woman agreed, smiling. "But I got them all the same." The harbormaster rang the noon bell, and across the water the washing on Gull Row lifted and billowed, bright as sails.

## Hop 58

That evening the three of them ate in the lighthouse kitchen, where the window looked out over the darkening harbor. The old woman, whose name was Agnes, had brought more than bread: a jar of plum preserves, a paper twist of salt, and a small brass key wrapped in a handkerchief. She set the key beside Tobias's plate and said nothing about it. Mira lit the lamp above the table and then climbed to light the great lamp above them all, counting the steps as she always did, ninety-one, ninety-two. When she came back down, Tobias was turning the key over in his fingers. "It's for the sea chest," Agnes said at last. "The one your father kept under the stairs. I took the key when I left. I thought if I had it, I would have to come back." "It took you thirty years," Tobias said, not unkindly. "It took me thirty years to be brave enough to be forgiven." Outside, the beam swept across the water, touching the moored boats one by one and letting them go. Mira cleared the plates slowly so she would not have to leave the room. Agnes caught her eye and nodded toward the empty chair, and Mira sat. Tobias stood, took the lamp from its hook, and went to the stairs. They heard the scrape of wood, the stubborn click of a lock that had waited a long time. Then silence, and then his voice, small and astonished, calling them both to come and see.

## Hop 59

The sea chest sat open at the foot of the stairs, its lid thrown back against the wall. Tobias knelt before it with the lamp held high, and in its trembling light Mira saw what had silenced him. The chest held no charts, no oilskins, no tarnished instruments. It held letters, hundreds of them, tied in bundles with faded blue ribbon, each envelope addressed in the same careful hand to Agnes, and none of them ever sent. "He wrote to you," Tobias said. "Every week, it looks like. For years." Agnes lowered herself onto the bottom step as though her knees had given way. She reached for the nearest bundle but did not untie it. Her fingers rested on the ribbon, tracing the knot. "He never knew where I'd gone," she whispered. "I made sure of that." "So he kept them here," Mira said softly, "where you'd find them if you ever came home." Beneath the letters lay one more thing: a child's wooden boat, its paint worn pale, a name carved into the hull. Tobias lifted it out and turned it toward the light. The name was his own. "He made this for me," he said. "I thought it was lost." Far above them, the great lamp kept turning, patient as it had always been. Agnes untied the first ribbon at last, unfolded the oldest letter, and began, in a voice that shook and then steadied, to read aloud.

## Hop 60

"My dearest Agnes," she read. "The storm has passed, and the boy is asleep at last. He asked for you again tonight. I told him you had gone across the water to see the lights of another harbor, and that one day you would come back and tell him about them." She paused, pressing the page flat against her knee. Tobias had gone very still, the little boat cradled in both hands as if it might break. "I don't remember asking," he said quietly. "You were three," Agnes answered, without looking up. "You wouldn't." Mira sat down beside the chest and began, carefully, to sort the bundles by the dates inked on each envelope. The ribbons were stiff with salt, and some of the paper had yellowed at the edges, but the words inside had kept. Winter after winter, the keeper had written about small things: the gulls that nested on the gallery rail, the year the supply boat came late, the first time Tobias climbed all one hundred and twelve steps without being carried. Never once did he write a word of blame. As Agnes read on, the wind outside softened, and the lamp's beam swept the dark water in long, slow circles. Somewhere near the fortieth letter, her voice broke entirely, and Tobias took the page from her gently and continued where she had stopped. Mira watched them, mother and son, bent together over the same unsteady light, and understood that the old keeper had finally finished the work he had begun. He had simply needed them both to come home.
