## Hop 1

The tide came in an hour early. Nobody in the village noticed at first, because the harbor clock had been running slow since the winter storms, and most people trusted the clock more than the sea. Only old Marta, who had gathered mussels on the flats every morning for forty years, felt something was wrong. She was halfway to the rocks when the water began curling around her ankles, cold and insistent, much sooner than it should have. She turned back, her basket only half full, and watched the channel fill with a speed that made her uneasy. By the time she reached the seawall, the fishing boats were already lifting off the mud, their ropes tightening like the strings of an instrument being tuned. Children who had been digging for crabs came running, shrieking with delight rather than fear, their buckets sloshing. Marta went straight to the harbormaster's hut and knocked until he opened the door, still holding his morning coffee. She told him the sea was ahead of itself. He laughed, then glanced at his tide tables, then at the water, and stopped laughing. They stood together on the wall as the gulls wheeled overhead, calling in sharp, broken voices. Something far out in the grey distance had shifted, a current or a sandbar or something neither of them could name. That afternoon, the whole village gathered on the headland to watch, and for the first time in years, they set their watches by the waves instead.

## Hop 2

The lighthouse keeper's daughter found the bottle on a Tuesday, wedged between two stones at the base of the cliff where the kelp gathered in thick, rubbery tangles. It was green glass, clouded by salt, and sealed with wax the color of old blood. Inside, curled tight, was a sheet of paper. She did not open it right away. Instead she carried it up the two hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room, where her father was polishing the great lens with a cloth soft as a rabbit's ear. He looked at the bottle for a long time without speaking. Then he set down his cloth and said that bottles like that one used to wash ashore when he was a boy, sent by sailors who wanted someone, anyone, to know they had existed. Most of them held names and dates. A few held love letters. One, he said, had held nothing but a single pressed flower, which his mother had kept in her Bible until the day she died. The girl broke the wax with her thumbnail. The paper unrolled reluctantly, brittle at the edges, and the ink had faded to the pale brown of weak tea. It was a map, hand-drawn, showing their own stretch of coast with a small cross marked on the northern headland. Beneath it, in careful letters, someone had written: for whoever keeps the light. Her father went very quiet. That evening, as the beam swept out across the darkening water, they packed a lantern and a shovel and began to walk north.

## Hop 3

The northern headland was farther than it looked from the lamp room, and by the time they reached it the moon had risen, thin and sharp as a fingernail paring. The wind came hard off the water, flattening the grass and pulling at the lantern flame until the girl cupped her hand around the glass to shelter it. Her father walked ahead with the shovel over his shoulder, counting paces under his breath the way he counted the turns of the clockwork that drove the lens. The map showed the cross set between a crooked hawthorn and a stone that looked, in the drawing, like a sleeping dog. They found the hawthorn first, bent almost double by a century of gales. The stone took longer. It was half buried in bracken, but when the lantern light fell across it, the girl laughed out loud, because it truly did look like a dog curled with its nose tucked under its tail. Her father began to dig. The soil was thin and full of roots, and twice the shovel rang against rock. The girl held the lantern steady and watched his breath smoke in the cold air. At last the blade struck something that was not stone. It was a tin box, rusted nearly through, no bigger than a loaf of bread. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a brass key and a photograph of a woman standing on the lighthouse gallery, one hand raised as if to wave. Her father stared at it for a long moment. Then, very softly, he said his mother's name.
