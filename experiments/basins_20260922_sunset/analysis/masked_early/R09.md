## Hop 1

Mara found the brass key beneath a loose floorboard in the room her grandfather had used as a workshop. It was smaller than her thumb, dark with age, and tied to a strip of blue ribbon. She had spent the morning sorting jars of screws and polishing tools, expecting nothing more mysterious than dust. The key changed her plans.

She searched the workshop for a lock it might fit. The cabinets were open, the drawers empty, and the door to the garden stood wide. At last, she noticed a narrow wooden panel behind the workbench. Its grain formed a crooked spiral, almost hidden beneath layers of varnish. When she pressed the center, the panel clicked inward.

Behind it was a shallow compartment containing a folded map and a packet of letters. The map showed the village as it had been decades earlier, before the railway crossed the river. A red line led from the workshop to a small island marked only with a star. On the first letter, her grandfather had written her name.

Mara sat on the floor and read. He described a place where he and her grandmother had gone whenever the world felt too loud: a willow-covered island with a spring that ran clear even in the driest summer. He had meant to take Mara there, but time had slipped away. The key, he wrote, opened the gate at the old footbridge.

By afternoon, Mara had packed the map and a bottle of water. She locked the workshop behind her and followed the river path, wondering what else her grandfather had left for her to discover.

## Hop 2

When the rain stopped, the town’s clock began running backward. At first, nobody noticed. Shopkeepers swept water from their doorways, children jumped over puddles, and the baker set fresh loaves in the window. Only the clockmaker, Ivo, saw its hands turning against the sun.

He climbed the tower stairs with a lantern and a set of tiny tools. Behind the clock face, he found a blue moth beating its wings against the gears. Its scales shimmered like the evening sky. Ivo reached toward it, but the moth slipped through a crack in the stone and vanished.

The hands stopped. Below, the town went quiet. A woman at the fountain stared at her empty bucket. The baker’s oven cooled, and a boy’s half-built paper boat sagged in the gutter. For one strange moment, everyone seemed to be waiting for something they had forgotten.

Ivo searched the tower until he found a narrow stair leading to a room beneath the clock. There, the blue moth rested on a wooden box. Inside lay a bundle of old photographs, each showing the town on the day its clock had first been built. In every picture, someone stood alone at the edge of the crowd.

The clockmaker understood. The moth had been feeding on memories left unshared. He carried the photographs to the square and began asking people who the strangers were. Stories followed: a lost sister, a brave teacher, a friend who had moved away.

As the townspeople spoke, the moth rose into the air. It circled the clock once, then flew toward the river. The hands started forward again. This time, everyone watched.

## Hop 3

On the first morning of winter, Mara found a door in the snow. It stood upright beside the old train tracks, painted green and fitted with a brass handle. There was no wall around it, only drifts of white stretching toward the pine forest.

She tugged the handle. Warm air breathed through the opening, carrying the smell of oranges and woodsmoke. Beyond the threshold lay a crowded station, bright with lamps. Travelers hurried across the platform, though every clock showed the same time: seven minutes past midnight.

A conductor in a red scarf approached. “You’re late,” he said, handing her a ticket with no destination printed on it. “The train leaves when you remember where you’re going.”

Mara thought of home: the narrow kitchen, her father’s silent chair, the suitcase she had packed after their argument. She had meant to leave before dawn, but the snow had covered the road. Now she stood with the ticket trembling in her glove.

A whistle sounded somewhere underground. The conductor waited. Mara could step aboard and let the train carry her away, or turn back through the green door. Neither choice felt easy.

Then, from the station’s far end, came a familiar tune—her father humming the song he used to sing while fixing the radio. Mara looked through the open doorway. On the snowy track, a lantern bobbed closer, and a figure called her name.

The ticket warmed in her hand. Its blank face filled with a single word: “Home.” Mara folded it carefully and stepped back into the winter night. Behind her, the train departed without a sound.
