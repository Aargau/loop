## Hop 1

The tide came in an hour early. By the time Mara reached the harbor, water was already licking the lowest steps, carrying bits of eelgrass and a bright red buoy that knocked softly against the stone. The fishermen had not returned, and the gulls circled above the empty slips as if waiting for someone to explain the change.

She set down her canvas bag and watched the water climb. Her grandfather used to say the sea kept its own clock, one that paid no attention to calendars or promises. When she was small, she thought he meant the moon. Now, standing alone beneath a sky the color of pewter, she wondered if he had meant something else entirely.

Across the harbor, the old signal bell began to ring. No one had touched it in years. Its notes drifted over the water, slow and uneven, while windows opened along the waterfront. A dog barked. Somewhere, a door slammed.

Mara pulled the folded chart from her coat. It was the one her grandfather had marked with pencil before he disappeared, though she had never understood the blue circle drawn around the lighthouse. The path to it was already underwater. She could turn back, call the coast guard, and wait for daylight—or take the narrow road above the cliffs before the rising water cut that off, too.

The bell rang again. Mara tucked the chart away and started toward the hill. Behind her, the harbor swallowed the first step, then the next, smoothing the stone as if it had never been there.

## Hop 2

At dawn, the railway station smelled of rain and burnt coffee. June stood beneath the departure board, watching the destinations flicker in and out as if the machine were trying to remember where the trains went. Her ticket was warm in her pocket from the long walk across town.

She had not told anyone she was leaving. Not her sister, who would have asked her to stay until the garden was planted, or Mr. Vale at the bakery, who saved the day-old rolls for her. Certainly not the old man in the blue house, who sat on his porch every evening and waved as though they had once been friends.

The train arrived with a sigh of brakes. Its windows reflected the platform lights, turning every passenger into a pale stranger. June stepped aboard and found a seat by the door. Across the aisle, a child pressed a paper boat against the glass. It had been folded from a page covered in careful handwriting.

“Is it yours?” June asked.

The child shook her head. “I found it under the bench.”

June took the boat gently. On its side, in faded ink, someone had written her name. Beneath it was an address she knew by heart: the blue house at the end of Birch Street.

Outside, the old man stood beside the tracks, hat lifted in one hand. He did not wave. Instead, he pointed toward the train, then tapped his wrist, though he wore no watch.

The doors began to close. June looked at the paper boat, then at the empty seat beside her. For the first time that morning, she wondered whether leaving and arriving might be the same journey, seen from opposite windows.

## Hop 3

By the time the ferry pulled away from the harbor, the fog had swallowed the town’s rooftops. Mara leaned against the rail and watched the last yellow windows disappear. In her coat pocket, a brass key pressed against her palm. She had carried it for twelve years without knowing what it opened.

The key had belonged to her father, who vanished one winter morning after promising to return before the first snow. He left no letter, only the key and a note that read, “When the tide is low.” For years, Mara had searched every shoreline near home, but the tide had never seemed low enough, or the right place had never revealed itself.

Now an envelope had arrived with no return address. Inside was a photograph of a stone doorway set into a cliff, and on the back, in her father’s unmistakable handwriting, were three words: “North island. Today.”

A bell rang, and the ferry’s deckhand pointed toward a shape emerging from the mist. The island was little more than a dark ridge, its cliffs striped with pale rock. As the boat approached, Mara saw a narrow beach below them, exposed by the receding water.

She stepped ashore with the key in one hand and the photograph in the other. At the far end of the beach, a doorway stood in the cliff. Its stone frame was worn smooth by salt and years.

The key turned easily. Beyond the door, a lantern glowed in the darkness. Someone had set two cups on a small table. One was still warm.
