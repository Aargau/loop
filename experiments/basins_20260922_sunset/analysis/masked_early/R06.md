## Hop 1

The sun was setting behind the hills, laying a copper ribbon across the river. Mara paused on the old wooden bridge and watched the current carry a yellow leaf beneath her feet. She had forgotten how quiet this place could be. In the city, even silence seemed to hum.

At the far end of the bridge, her grandfather waited with his hands tucked into the pockets of his coat. He had grown smaller since spring, though his hat was still the same battered green one he wore in every photograph.

“You took your time,” he said.

“I was looking.”

He nodded, as if that were a perfectly reasonable thing to spend an afternoon doing.

They walked toward the house along the narrow path beside the water. Somewhere in the reeds, a bird gave a sharp, questioning cry. Mara almost reached for her phone, then remembered there was no one she needed to answer tonight.

“Did you fix the kitchen clock?” she asked.

“No. Still ten past four.”

“It’s been ten past four for six years.”

“Reliable, isn’t it?”

She laughed, and he smiled without turning around.

The house appeared beyond the orchard, its windows bright against the gathering blue. A dish towel hung over the porch railing. Beside the steps, someone had planted new rosemary in the cracked stone pot her grandmother used to love.

Mara stopped to brush its leaves with her fingers. The scent rose clean and familiar.

Her grandfather held the door open. She followed him inside, carrying her bag to the chair where she had always left it.

## Hop 2

By morning, the storm had left the harbor full of strange things: a red bucket, half a gate, a wooden chair floating upright as though someone might sit down.

Elias found the violin case caught beneath the lowest rung of the pier ladder.

He hauled it up carefully. Water streamed from its seams onto his boots. Inside, the blue lining was soaked, but there was no violin—only a folded map and a pair of woolen gloves.

“Anything worth keeping?” called June from her boat.

“Depends who lost it.”

She climbed onto the pier and crouched beside him. One glove had been repaired with yellow thread. June touched the stitching, then withdrew her hand.

“My mother used to do that,” she said. “Said matching thread was a waste of daylight.”

Elias unfolded the map against his knee. It showed the coast as it had been decades earlier, before the road reached the lighthouse. Someone had circled a cove north of the village.

June looked toward the breakwater.

“There was a house there once.”

“Whose?”

She took a moment to answer. “Ours.”

Around them, the harbor resumed its ordinary noises: ropes tapping masts, an engine coughing awake, gulls quarreling over something invisible.

Elias offered her the gloves. She pressed the water from them between her palms and laid them across the pier railing.

“The path might still be there,” she said.

He glanced at the damaged boats, the debris collecting against the pilings. There was work enough for days.

“I can get my truck,” he said.

June nodded and went aboard for her coat.

## Hop 3

The library clock stopped at eleven seventeen, though nobody noticed until nearly noon. Ruth was sorting returned books when a boy approached the desk carrying a shoebox.

“I think this belongs here,” he said.

Inside lay thirty or forty borrowing cards, their corners softened by use. Each bore the same name: Arthur Bell.

“Where did you find them?”

“In my grandfather’s shed. Mum said throw them away.”

Ruth lifted the first card. A gardening manual, borrowed in March 1978. Then a book about beekeeping. Then three volumes of poetry, all returned late.

She remembered Arthur as a man who kept his hat on indoors and always asked whether the windows could be opened.

“Was he a big reader?” the boy asked.

“Apparently.”

“He never read anything at our house.”

Beneath the cards was a photograph of the library garden before it became a parking lot. Arthur stood beside a young woman holding a spade. Ruth recognized her own coat before she recognized herself.

The boy leaned closer.

“Do you know who that is?”

“Yes,” she said. “We planted a pear tree.”

“Is it still there?”

Ruth glanced through the window. At the far edge of the asphalt, a narrow trunk rose from a square of earth. Every autumn, someone complained about the fallen fruit.

She turned the photograph over. Arthur had written the date, nothing else.

“Come outside,” she said.

She fetched the office key and led him through the side door. Beside the tree, the boy looked from the photograph to the branches, then stepped back to find the place where his grandfather had stood.
