## Hop 1

The tide came in an hour early. Marguerite noticed it first from the kitchen window, the way the water had already swallowed the flat gray stones where the gulls usually stood at this time of morning. She checked the almanac pinned beside the stove, ran her finger down the column of dates, and frowned. The numbers were right. The sea was wrong.

She pulled on her boots and walked down to the shingle. The air smelled of iodine and something sharper, like a struck match. Out past the breakwater, the water lay unusually still, a sheet of pewter without the small chop that normally ruffled it. The fishing boats had not gone out. She could see them at their moorings, bobbing in a slow, uneasy rhythm, and along the harbor wall a handful of men stood with their hands in their pockets, saying nothing.

Old Teodor was among them. He had fished these waters for sixty years and claimed he could feel weather in his knees before the radio announced it. When Marguerite reached him he did not turn, only nodded toward the horizon.

"Twice in my life," he said. "Once in fifty-three. Once the winter my brother drowned."

"What does it mean?"

He shrugged, but his jaw was tight. "Means the sea has changed its mind about something."

By noon the water had climbed the lower steps of the church. The children were kept indoors. Marguerite moved her mother's photographs to the upper shelf and waited, listening to the strange, patient lapping at the door.

## Hop 2

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Hollis would later think of as the last ordinary day. It came in a plain cream envelope with no return address, and the handwriting on the front was so familiar that his hand shook before his mind had caught up to why.

He set it on the kitchen table and made coffee he did not drink. Outside, the neighbor's sprinkler ticked across the lawn in its usual arc. A dog barked twice and stopped. Everything insisted on being normal.

The handwriting belonged to his sister. She had been dead for eleven years.

Hollis knew the loops of her capital H, the way she crossed her sevens like a European, the slight leftward lean that their mother had tried and failed to correct. He had kept every birthday card she ever sent him in a shoebox in the closet, and he did not need to take them out to compare.

When he finally opened it, the paper inside was a single sheet, folded once. There was no date. It said only: *I'm sorry it took so long. Meet me where we buried the tin.*

The tin. A biscuit tin, painted with faded roses, that they had filled with marbles and a plastic dinosaur and a note to their future selves, then sunk into the soft earth beneath the willow at their grandmother's farm. The farm had been sold in ninety-eight. The willow, for all he knew, was gone.

Hollis read the note four times. Then he took his keys from the hook by the door, and did not stop to lock it behind him.

## Hop 3

The drive took four hours, most of it on roads Hollis had not traveled since he was a boy. He did not turn on the radio. He watched the fields flatten and then rise again into the low hills of the county where his grandmother had lived, and he tried not to think about what he was doing, because thinking about it made it impossible.

The farm had a new name on the gate. Someone had painted the barn a hard modern red and put up a satellite dish. But the willow was still there, enormous now, its branches sweeping the ground like a woman gathering her skirts. He parked on the shoulder and walked across the field without asking anyone's permission.

The earth beneath the tree was dry and packed. He had brought nothing to dig with. He knelt and used his hands, and then a flat stone, and the light went from gold to gray while he worked. His fingernails split. He kept going.

The tin was deeper than he remembered. When the stone struck metal, he stopped breathing for a moment, then clawed the rest of the dirt away and lifted it out. The roses had faded almost to nothing. The lid was rusted shut.

He sat back on his heels and held it in his lap. Behind him, footsteps came through the grass, slow and even, the way she had always walked when she wanted him to turn around first.

Hollis did not turn. He closed his eyes and waited, and the footsteps stopped just close enough.
