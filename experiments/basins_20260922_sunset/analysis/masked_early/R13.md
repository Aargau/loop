## Hop 1

The lighthouse keeper climbed the spiral staircase for the last time that season, counting each iron step as he had done for nearly thirty years. One hundred and twelve. The number never changed, but his knees told him it grew larger every winter. At the top, the great lens sat silent beneath its canvas cover, waiting for the automated system that would replace him in the spring. He pulled the cover away and ran his palm along the cool glass prisms, remembering the storms they had cut through, the fishing boats they had guided home, and the single night a freighter had nearly struck the rocks before turning at the final moment. Outside, the sea was calm and gray, stretching toward a horizon softened by mist. Gulls circled below, calling to one another in voices that sounded almost like laughter. He had kept a logbook every day of his service, and the shelves downstairs now held forty-three volumes filled with weather reports, ship sightings, and small observations no one would ever read: the morning a whale surfaced near the jetty, the afternoon his daughter visited with her newborn son, the evening the power failed and he turned the lens by hand until dawn. He opened the final volume and wrote the date carefully. Beneath it, after a long pause, he added a single line: The light will go on without me, as it should. Then he closed the book, replaced the cover over the lens, and began the long walk down, counting each step again.

## Hop 2

The baker unlocked the shop at four in the morning, as she had every day since her grandmother handed her the keys. The bell above the door gave its familiar tired jingle, and the smell of yesterday's bread still hung in the air like a memory refusing to leave. She tied on her apron, switched on the ovens, and listened to them tick as they warmed. Flour dusted the wooden counter where three generations of hands had kneaded dough, and a shallow groove had formed near the center, worn smooth by decades of patient pressing. She measured water, salt, and yeast without looking at the scale, trusting the weight of the bowl in her arms. Outside, the street was dark and empty except for a delivery truck rumbling past and a cat watching from the windowsill across the road. By five, the first loaves were rising beneath linen towels, swelling slowly like sleeping animals breathing. She thought about the letter on the kitchen table upstairs, the one from the developer offering more money than the shop had earned in twenty years. She had read it four times and still had not answered. When the timer sounded, she pulled a tray of golden rolls from the oven and set them on the rack to cool. The crust crackled softly as it met the cooler air, a sound her grandmother used to call the bread singing. She stood still and listened until it faded. Then she turned the sign on the door to open, poured herself a cup of coffee, and waited for the neighborhood to wake.

## Hop 3

The lighthouse keeper climbed the spiral stairs at dusk, counting each of the one hundred and twelve steps the way his father had taught him. The iron railing was cold beneath his palm, and the wind pressed against the tower walls with a low, steady moan. At the top, he wiped salt from the thick glass and checked the brass fittings, polishing a smudge that no ship would ever notice. The lamp had been automated nine years ago, and a technician from the mainland visited twice a year to make sure the sensors still worked. No one needed him here anymore. Still, he came every evening, because the light seemed lonelier without someone to watch it turn. Below, the sea churned gray and restless, throwing white spray against the rocks where a fishing boat had broken apart long before he was born. He kept a small notebook in his coat pocket and wrote down the weather each night: wind direction, cloud cover, the color of the water. The pages were filling faster now, and he wondered who would read them when he was gone. His daughter called on Sundays and asked when he would move to the city to live with her family. He always said soon. Tonight the clouds broke apart just as the lamp began to sweep, and a thin slice of moon appeared above the horizon. He watched the beam pass over the waves, again and again, steady as a heartbeat. Then he sat on the narrow bench, opened his notebook, and wrote a single word: clear.
