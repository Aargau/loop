## Hop 1

The sun was setting behind the hills, spilling amber light across the valley like honey poured from a tipped jar. Long shadows stretched from the fence posts and the old oak by the creek, reaching toward the farmhouse as if they, too, wanted to come inside before dark. Mara stood on the porch with a mug of tea cooling in her hands, watching the sky shift from gold to rose to a soft, bruised violet. Somewhere in the tall grass, crickets began their evening chorus, tentative at first, then swelling until the whole field seemed to hum. A pair of swallows stitched quick loops above the barn, catching the last insects of the day. The air smelled of cut hay and damp earth, carrying the faint sweetness of the honeysuckle that climbed the trellis beside the steps. She thought of her grandmother, who had stood on this same porch for fifty years, marking the seasons by where the sun slipped below the ridge. In summer it vanished near the twin pines; in winter, far to the south, behind the quarry. Her grandmother used to say that a sunset was the day's way of saying thank you, and that it was rude not to watch. Mara had laughed at that as a child. Now, older and quieter, she understood. As the first star pricked through the deepening blue, a breeze stirred the wind chimes, and she whispered a small thank you of her own before turning to go inside.

## Hop 2

The first snow arrived overnight, quiet as a held breath, and by morning the town had been rewritten in white. Elias woke before his alarm, sensing the change in the light before he saw it, that pale brightness that seeps around curtains and hushes a room. He padded to the window and found the street transformed: parked cars softened into rounded dunes, mailboxes wearing tall white caps, the maple in the neighbor's yard traced branch by branch in delicate frost. No tire tracks yet marred the road. No footprints crossed the sidewalk. The world looked unused, as if it had been unwrapped just for him. He pulled on his boots and his father's old wool coat, the one that still smelled faintly of pipe smoke and cedar, and stepped outside. The cold caught in his chest, sharp and clean. Each step made a muffled crunch, and he found himself walking slowly, reluctant to spoil too much of the smooth surface at once. A cardinal flared red against the hedge, then vanished. Down the block, a door opened and a small child tumbled out, shrieking with joy, followed by a dog that bounded in wild circles. Elias smiled. When he was young, his father had taught him to catch snowflakes on his tongue and to listen for the particular silence that only snowfall brings. He tilted his head back now, letting a few flakes land on his face, and listened. The silence was there, just as he remembered, wide and gentle, holding everything still.

## Hop 3

By late afternoon the snow had turned the color of weak tea, and the light slanted low across the rooftops as if it, too, were tired. Mara stood at the edge of the frozen pond with a thermos cradled in her mittens, watching the skaters trace their looping signatures across the ice. Some glided with effortless grace, arms folded behind their backs like herons; others wobbled and flailed, laughing each time they fell. An old man in a knitted cap moved slowly along the far shore, sweeping a path with a wide broom, pausing now and then to wave at children who called his name. The air smelled of woodsmoke and cold metal. Somewhere behind her, a vendor was selling roasted chestnuts, and the scent drifted over in warm, sweet ribbons. Mara unscrewed the thermos and poured cocoa into the lid, steam curling up and fogging her glasses for a moment. She had not skated since she was twelve, the winter her grandmother had held her hands and pulled her gently backward across this very pond, promising she would not let go. She had kept that promise until Mara no longer needed it. Now the skates hung in her closet, laces stiff with age, blades dulled by years of waiting. She sipped the cocoa and considered them. The sun slipped lower, gilding the ice with streaks of amber and rose. A girl nearby took her first shaky strides, clutching her mother's sleeve. Mara watched, then smiled, and decided that tomorrow she would bring the skates.
