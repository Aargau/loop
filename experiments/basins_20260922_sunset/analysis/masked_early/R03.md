## Hop 1

The tide came in an hour early. Mara noticed because the water reached the third step of the harbor stairs while the church clock was still striking four. She had spent forty years watching that staircase disappear and return, and the sea had always kept its appointments.

She closed her bait shop and walked toward the breakwater. Gulls circled above the fishing boats, which rocked against their ropes. Beyond them, the horizon had gone strangely flat. There was no storm cloud, no wind strong enough to trouble her coat. Only the water moved, slipping over the lowest stones and into the street.

At the pier, young Daniel was hauling in his nets. Mara called for him to leave them. He glanced at the clock, then at the water, and dropped the line.

They went door to door along the waterfront. Most people followed them uphill. A few stopped to gather photographs or look for a missing cat. By the time everyone reached the square, seawater was running past the shop windows, carrying a crate of oranges and a red plastic chair.

Mara sat on the church steps beside Daniel and counted the boats. She could see all six, their bows lifting as the water rose. Then the harbor bell rang once, though no one was near it.

The water stayed in the streets until after dark. When it finally receded, Mara went down with a torch to check the shop. A line of salt marked the counter. Her clock had stopped at four, and someone had left a wet orange on the doorstep.

## Hop 2

By morning, the orange had dried to a dull shine. Mara set it on the counter while she swept sand from beneath the shelves. Nothing else in the shop had moved, though the water had been high enough to float the tins of bait. They stood in neat rows, their labels facing the door.

Daniel arrived carrying a bucket and a loaf of bread. He had been checking the boats. All six were sound, he said, but every compass aboard pointed toward the church.

Mara thought of the bell. Together they climbed the hill, passing neighbors who were washing salt from their windows. The church door was unlocked. Inside, the air smelled of damp stone, and a narrow trail of sand crossed the floor to the bell rope. At its end lay a second orange.

Daniel picked it up. A small square of paper had been pressed into the peel, leaving its corners outlined in white. Mara eased it free. On one side was a drawing of the harbor stairs. On the other, in careful handwriting, were three words: Look beneath them.

They waited for the tide to turn. At noon the bottom step emerged, slick with weed. Daniel knelt and reached into the gap below it. His hand closed around a brass key tied to a length of blue string.

Mara recognized the string. She sold it by the yard to fishermen, but her father had used the same kind to mark things he meant to find again. She took the key from Daniel and looked uphill at the locked door of his old house.

## Hop 3

The key turned stiffly in the lock. Daniel pushed the door open, and the smell of dust met them. His father had kept the house shut since Mara’s father died, though no one had ever explained why.

In the kitchen, every chair stood upside down on the table. Blue string ran from one chair leg to another, making a crooked web. At its center hung a tin of bait, its label facing the door.

Daniel took it down. Inside was a folded chart of the harbor, marked with six small crosses. Each cross lay beside a mooring.

“The boats,” he said.

On the back, Mara found her father’s handwriting: When the bell rings without a hand, move them beyond the breakwater.

A single note sounded from the church. Through the window, Mara saw neighbors stop in the street and turn toward the hill.

Daniel was already reaching for the chart, but Mara held it flat against the table. The crosses were not where the six boats lay now. They marked the deeper moorings used before the harbor stairs had been rebuilt.

“The tide took the stairs once,” she said. “He thought it might happen again.”

Outside, water spilled over the quay, though the tide should have been falling. Daniel ran to warn the boat owners. Mara followed with the chart, calling out the old moorings as they went. By the time the church bell rang again, the first boat had slipped its rope and was heading for open water.
