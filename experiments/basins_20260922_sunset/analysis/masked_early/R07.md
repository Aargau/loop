## Hop 1

The sun was setting behind the hills, and the last of its light spilled across the valley like something poured from a cup. Mara stood at the edge of the orchard with her hands in her coat pockets, watching the shadows lengthen between the apple trees. She had come out here every evening for a week now, though she could not have said exactly why. The house behind her was warm and full of the smell of bread, and her brother would be wondering where she had gone.

Still, there was something in this hour that asked to be witnessed. The birds had quieted. A single cow lowed from the Hendersons' field across the road, and then even that fell silent. The hills turned from gold to violet to a deep, settled blue, and one by one the stars began to open above them.

Her grandmother used to say that dusk was the seam of the day, the place where two cloths were stitched together and you could see the thread if you looked closely enough. Mara had thought it a silly thing to say when she was young. Now she understood it a little better. Everything felt briefly unfinished at this hour, as though the world were holding its breath before deciding what it would become.

A light came on in the kitchen window. Her brother's silhouette moved past it, then paused, then moved again. She smiled, took one last look at the darkening ridge, and turned back toward home, the grass whispering softly against her boots.

## Hop 2

The morning fog had not yet lifted from the harbor when Tomas pushed the boat out from the dock. The water was flat and gray, the color of old pewter, and the oars dipped into it with a sound like someone clearing their throat. He rowed slowly, not because he was tired but because there was no reason to hurry. The fish would be where they always were, or they would not, and either way the day would unfold as it pleased.

His father had taught him to read the water when he was seven. Look for the ripples that move against the wind, the old man had said, and the gulls that circle without landing. Tomas had believed, back then, that these were secrets only fishermen knew. Later he understood that the harbor gave its signs freely to anyone patient enough to sit still and watch.

The fog began to thin as he reached the outer buoys. First the church steeple appeared above the town, then the red roofs below it, then the long curve of the seawall with its line of sleeping boats. Somewhere a bell rang once and stopped. A cormorant surfaced beside him, regarded him with an expression of mild disappointment, and dove again.

Tomas let the boat drift. He baited his lines and dropped them over the side, watching the weights sink until they vanished into the green dark. Then he leaned back against the gunwale and waited, as his father had waited, and his grandfather before that, while the sun climbed unhurried out of the sea and the whole world slowly turned to gold.

## Hop 3

By midday the wind had shifted to the south, and Tomas felt the change before he saw it. The lines went slack, then taut, then slack again, as though something below were testing his patience rather than his bait. He did not mind. He ate the bread and cheese his wife had wrapped in a cloth that morning, and drank water from a bottle warmed by the sun, and watched the shadows of clouds slide across the seafloor where it rose into shallows near the point.

A second boat appeared from behind the headland, low and dark, its engine muttering. Tomas raised one hand and the man at the tiller raised his in return. Neither called out. There was an understanding among those who worked the harbor that the water was large enough for all of them and that silence was a kind of courtesy. The boat passed and its wake reached him a full minute later, lifting and settling him like a slow breath.

The first fish came just after one o'clock, a mackerel that flashed silver and blue as it broke the surface. Tomas brought it in with the same unhurried motion his father had used, unhooked it, and laid it in the wet bottom of the boat where it drummed briefly and then lay still. Three more followed within the hour, and then nothing.

He did not wait for more. He pulled the lines, shipped the oars, and turned toward the seawall, where the red roofs had gone pale in the afternoon light and someone had begun, faintly, to hammer.
