## Hop 1

The tide came in an hour early. By the time Mara noticed, water had swallowed the causeway and lifted the loose boards beneath her father's abandoned boathouse.

She stood in the doorway with a brass key in her palm. For twenty years, he had told her it opened nothing worth finding. Then he died, and the key arrived in an envelope without a letter.

Inside, the boathouse smelled of salt, old rope, and something sweeter: the pipe tobacco he had supposedly given up before she was born. A green cabinet leaned against the far wall. Its lock turned easily.

There were no charts inside, no money, no explanation. Only a pair of small red boots and a notebook swollen with damp.

Mara recognized the boots from a photograph of her older sister, taken the summer before she disappeared.

Outside, a wave struck the steps.

The notebook contained dates, tide heights, and the same sentence repeated in her father's careful handwriting: She comes back when the water forgets its time.

Mara read it twice. Then she heard footsteps beneath the floorboards, light and impatient, moving toward the ladder at the rear of the building.

She should have run. Later, she would wonder whether she ever truly had that choice.

Instead, she set the boots beside the open hatch.

The footsteps stopped.

From below came a voice she remembered only from home movies, bright with annoyance.

“Those won't fit me anymore.”

Mara knelt as the water rose around her shoes.

“Then we'll get you new ones,” she said, and reached into the dark.

## Hop 2

Every Thursday, the library returned a book that nobody had borrowed.

It appeared in the overnight slot at six, bound in gray cloth, its pages warm enough to steam in winter. Eleanor always shelved it in Local History. By closing time, it was gone.

For eleven years, she had respected this arrangement.

Then the demolition notice arrived.

On the last Thursday, Eleanor waited beside the slot with a flashlight and her coat buttoned wrong. At six precisely, the flap lifted.

A small hand pushed the book through.

She caught the wrist before she could reconsider.

“Your library card,” she said.

The hand went still.

“We don't have those anymore,” a child replied.

Eleanor opened the service door.

Beyond it stood a girl beneath an enormous white sky. Where the parking lot should have been, pale grass bent around the foundations of buildings Eleanor almost recognized.

The girl glanced past her at the shelves.

“Is it true?” she asked. “You're closing?”

“Tomorrow.”

“My grandmother said this was the last door.”

Eleanor looked down at the book. For the first time, its cover bore a title: A Catalogue of Things We Managed to Save.

She opened it. The first page described rain. The second contained a recipe for bread. Farther on were instructions for setting a broken arm.

“How much time do you have?” Eleanor asked.

The girl looked over her shoulder. Something distant was lifting dust into the white sky.

Eleanor propped the door open with a dictionary and fetched the returns cart.

“Start with the medical section,” she said. “I'll find you something to carry them in.”

## Hop 3

The sea began returning my father's tools three days after his funeral.

First came the hammer, laid neatly above the tideline. Then his folding ruler. On Friday, I found the red-handled screwdriver he'd accused me of losing when I was twelve.

I carried them home and rinsed the salt away.

My mother watched from the kitchen window.

“Don't thank it,” she said.

She had lived beside the water longer than I had.

By Monday, the beach was littered with objects: fishing hooks, spectacles, a wedding ring neither of us recognized. Everything smelled faintly of his workshop.

Then came the door.

It stood upright in the sand, painted the same green as our old house, with a brass number seven screwed crookedly beneath the knocker.

Our old house had burned down twenty years earlier.

Behind the door, someone coughed.

I knew that cough. The apologetic little rattle before he asked for help with something he could no longer lift alone.

My hand found the knob.

My mother caught my sleeve.

“Your father never knocked at his own door.”

The coughing stopped.

We stood listening to the waves.

“What does it want?” I whispered.

From the other side came the scrape of a chair being pulled close.

My mother took the screwdriver from my pocket. She knelt beside the lowest hinge and began to work.

“Hold it steady.”

Inside, my father's voice said her name.

She paused, her forehead pressed against the wood. Then she turned the screwdriver again.

I held the door while she worked. When the last hinge came loose, we laid it flat in the sand and walked home.
