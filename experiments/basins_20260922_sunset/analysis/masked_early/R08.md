## Hop 1

The sun was setting behind the hills, leaving a narrow ribbon of gold along the horizon. Mara paused beside the old stone bridge and watched the river gather the evening into its darkening current. She had not stood here in twenty years, yet the place seemed to remember her: the leaning willow, the broken milestone, the smell of rain rising from warm earth.

In her coat pocket lay a key wrapped in a handkerchief. Her brother had mailed it without explanation, along with a note that said only, Come before the first frost.

Now the village windows were beginning to glow. Somewhere beyond the church, a dog barked twice, and a bicycle rattled over the cobblestones. Ordinary sounds, but they made her throat tighten. She had spent so long imagining this return that she had forgotten it would happen on an ordinary evening, among people thinking about supper.

Mara crossed the bridge. At the far end, she noticed a small wooden boat caught between two reeds. Its paper sail had collapsed, but a blue stripe remained visible along the hull. Her brother used to paint their toy boats that exact color.

She bent to free it, then stopped. Written across the little deck, in familiar crooked letters, was her name.

From the road above came footsteps.

“You took your time,” her brother said.

Mara straightened slowly. He was holding a second boat, its white sail bright against his sleeve.

“I wasn’t sure you’d come,” he added.

She lifted the dripping boat. “Neither was I.”

## Hop 2

Every Thursday, the clockmaker found a pear on his doorstep. No basket, no note, just one green pear resting precisely in the center of the mat.

At first, Emil assumed it was a mistake. He left the fruit on the wall beside his shop, where schoolchildren usually claimed anything edible. But the following Thursday another appeared, its stem tied with red thread.

He asked the baker, the postman, the woman who swept the station steps. Nobody admitted knowing anything.

By November, he had collected six lengths of thread in a drawer meant for watch springs.

On the seventh Thursday, he opened the shop early and waited behind the curtain. At half past five, a girl in an oversized yellow coat approached. She placed a pear on the mat, adjusted it carefully, and turned away.

Emil opened the door.

“Have we met?” he asked.

The girl shook her head. “You fixed my grandfather’s clock.”

“I fix many clocks.”

“This one had birds inside. You wouldn’t take his money.”

Emil remembered the old man carrying the clock wrapped in a blanket. He had died before spring.

“He said you should always pay people,” the girl continued. “Our tree did well this year.”

Emil looked at the pear. There was a small bruise near its base, shaped like a thumbprint.

“Would you like to hear the clocks strike six?” he asked.

She glanced toward the empty street, then nodded.

Inside, he found a knife and two saucers. While the minute hands climbed, they ate the pear in silence, leaving the red thread beside the seeds.

## Hop 3

The library’s last visitor always arrived five minutes before closing. He wore a raincoat even in dry weather and returned a book he had never borrowed.

Mara checked the records twice. The books belonged to the library, but their cards were blank, their covers unfamiliar. One described bridges demolished before she was born. Another contained recipes written entirely in the future tense.

“You’ll make a good soup,” it began.

On Friday, the man brought a thin blue atlas.

“This one is overdue,” he said.

Inside, Mara found a map of her childhood street. The corner shop was there, and the chestnut tree, and the narrow house her mother had sold after the funeral. In the garden stood a tiny square marked SUMMERHOUSE.

“We never had a summerhouse,” she said.

“Are you certain?”

Her father had bought timber one April. She remembered it stacked beneath a tarpaulin, remembered how the rain collected in the folds. Then he became ill, and someone took the timber away.

Mara touched the square.

“What is this place?”

“Something that nearly was.”

Outside, a bus sighed at the curb. The man glanced toward it.

“Can I borrow this?” she asked.

“You work here.”

After he left, Mara carried the atlas to the desk. She made a card, stamped a return date, and wrote her name carefully on the line.

At home, she opened it again. Through the summerhouse window, no larger than a grain of rice, she could see a yellow chair.

She remembered choosing the paint. Her father had wanted green, but she had insisted.
