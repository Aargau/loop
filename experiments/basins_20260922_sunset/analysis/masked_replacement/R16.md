## Hop 1

The sun was setting behind the hills when Mara reached the abandoned station. Its clock had stopped at 4:17, though the hands still cast long shadows across the cracked face. She set her suitcase on the platform and listened. Wind moved through the grass beside the tracks. Somewhere inside the station, a door tapped gently against its frame.

Her father had told her to meet him here if she ever needed to know why he left. For years, she had kept his note folded in a kitchen drawer, beneath batteries and spare keys. That morning, after finding the drawer empty except for the note, she had finally bought a ticket.

The station door opened with a shove. Inside, dust softened the edges of the benches. A timetable hung behind the counter, listing towns she had never heard of. On the nearest bench sat a small blue tin with her name written on the lid.

She opened it. There was a photograph of her father as a boy, standing beside a woman who looked remarkably like Mara. Beneath it lay a train ticket dated tomorrow and a second note, written in the same careful hand: I hoped you would come. I am sorry I could not wait.

Mara read the words twice. Then she turned the photograph over. On the back, her father had written the woman's name and an address in a town three stops away.

Outside, a light appeared far down the track. Mara closed the tin and picked up her suitcase. This time, she would not let the train pass.

## Hop 2

The lighthouse had been dark for twenty years, but every evening its keeper climbed the stairs. He carried no oil, only a brass key and a notebook swollen with damp. At the top, he unlocked the lantern room, wiped salt from the glass, and wrote down the weather.

Tonight, the sea was calm. That worried him.

From the window, Elias could see the fishing boats returning to the harbor, each with a lamp glowing at its bow. Beyond them lay the reef where his brother’s boat had vanished. On the night of the wreck, the lighthouse had been shining. Elias had checked the mechanism himself.

He opened his notebook to the first page. Beneath the date of the wreck, in handwriting he did not recognize, someone had written: Ask what the light was for.

A knock came from below. Elias descended to find a young woman waiting at the door, her coat dripping seawater onto the stone floor. She held up a photograph of the lighthouse taken from a boat. In it, two beams crossed the water: one from the tower and another, brighter one, from somewhere on shore.

“My mother took this,” she said. “She was on the boat behind your brother’s.”

Elias studied the shoreline in the photograph. The second beam came from the old customs house. Its windows had been boarded up the morning after the wreck.

Outside, the harbor bells began to ring. Elias took his coat from its hook and followed the woman into the rain. For the first time in twenty years, he left the lantern room unlocked.

## Hop 3

The customs house stood above the harbor, its boarded windows silvered by rain. Elias had passed it every day for twenty years. Tonight, following the young woman up the path, he noticed fresh footprints in the mud.

“My name is Mara,” she said. “My mother kept the photograph hidden until she died last week.”

At the door, someone had scraped a circle in the paint around the lock. Elias tried his brass key. It turned.

Inside, the rooms smelled of wet timber. They found a lamp beneath a sheet, its glass lens aimed through a narrow gap between two boards. Beside it lay a ledger of ships and cargo. Elias found his brother’s boat listed on the night it vanished, though it had carried no cargo at all.

A voice sounded behind them. “You should have let the sea keep its secrets.”

The harbor master stood in the doorway, older than Elias remembered him ever being. He held a box of matches, but his hands shook too badly to strike one.

Mara lifted the ledger. “Who paid you?”

He looked at Elias. “Your brother found out about the smuggling. We lit a false channel to draw him onto the reef. I thought the boat behind him would turn back.”

For a moment Elias heard only rain on the roof. Then the harbor bells rang again, closer now, and voices called from the path.

He took the ledger from Mara and stepped outside to meet them. Across the water, the lighthouse lantern room stood open to the storm. By morning, he decided, it would have a light again.

## Hop 10

The merchant’s boat came alongside the customs steps instead of the public quay. One of the crew threw a rope up, but no one on shore reached for it. The constable stood at the top of the steps with his officers on either side.

“Stay aboard, Mr. Venn,” he called.

The merchant looked toward Elias. “I’ve brought your brother’s things.”

Elias took a step forward. Mara kept hold of his sleeve, though he no longer seemed ready to run. Venn lifted a canvas bundle from beneath the bow seat and set it on the boards. Even from across the water, Elias recognized the red cord tied around it.

“Where is he?” Elias asked.

Venn did not answer. The man at the tiller stared at his hands.

The constable went down the steps alone. He untied the rope and secured the boat, then asked Venn to come ashore. Venn glanced once at the harbor mouth before obeying.

Elias crossed to meet them. Ada and Mara followed. The bundle held a coat, a pair of worn boots, and a small notebook swollen with seawater. Elias opened it carefully. Most of the ink had run, but a date remained legible on the last page. Beneath it, his brother had written three names.

Venn saw the page and went pale. “He was alive when we left him,” he said.

The constable turned to him. “Left him where?”

Across the harbor, the tide was falling. Venn looked at the exposed rocks beyond the reef, then pointed toward the old signal station.

## Hop 20

The footprints crossed the room one at a time. Each left a dark print on the boards before the next appeared.

Ada moved toward the door. The iron key tipped in its lock, then straightened. Beneath the brass plate came three knocks and two more.

The constable caught her sleeve. “That’s how it asks to be let through.”

Venn’s wife had not moved. She stared at the prints approaching her. “My husband used to knock like that,” she said. “When he came home late.”

The lantern guttered. In its brief darkness, water pattered onto the floor. When the flame recovered, a wet handprint marked the wall above the stove, too high for any of them to reach.

Elias seized the poker. “What happens if we pull the key out?”

“I don’t know,” said the constable.

The footprints stopped before Venn’s wife. She bent and pressed her fingers into the nearest one. When she lifted them, they were dry.

“Not him,” she said.

A blow shook the floor. The brass plate rose against its screws. Ada dropped to her knees and gripped the key before it could turn. Its warmth had gone; it burned cold against her palm.

“Get upstairs,” she told the others.

They crowded toward the narrow stair. The constable stayed, holding the lantern over her shoulder. Its light caught scratches around the keyhole: dozens of sets of five notches, cut into the brass from below.

Ada held the key steady. Somewhere under the boards, another key began to turn.

## Hop 30

Ada left the pen where it lay. On the page, a third blank line appeared.

“How many names did you cross out?” Elias asked.

Venn looked toward the ceiling. “Everyone who ever lived in this house.”

The floorboards creaked. Mara’s voice, coming from upstairs now, said, “Everyone except us.”

The real Mara caught Elias by the sleeve before he could answer. She turned the blackened paper over. On its back, beneath a smear of ash, someone had written a date: tomorrow’s.

The constable reached for the ledger. Venn seized his wrist. “Don’t touch a name.”

Above them, the kitchen door banged open. Boots crossed the floor, four deliberate steps, then stopped over the hatch.

Ada picked up the pen by its nib. Its barrel was warm. She pressed it to the blank line and wrote HOUSE.

The nib split. Ink spread through the paper, filling the cellar with the smell of rain on hot stone. Upstairs, every footstep broke into a run. Something struck the kitchen door hard enough to shake dust from the joists.

The ledger snapped shut. Venn recoiled, but Ada held her ground as black threads crept from its cover toward her shoe. The key in Mara’s hand flared white. For an instant, Ada saw the threads clearly: not ink, but tiny lines of handwriting, each spelling a name.

Mara brought the key down on the ledger’s spine. The threads vanished. So did the light.

In the dark, someone at the top of the stairs began to laugh in the constable’s voice.

## Hop 40

Ada held the ring toward the light. Soot filled the tiny strokes of Mara’s name.

The screaming stopped. In the silence, Venn’s boots found purchase, and Elias dragged him clear of the splinter. The fingertip withdrew, leaving a black crescent in the wood.

“Mara was my daughter,” the constable said. The line had reached his shoulder. He kept his palm against the floor, though his arm trembled. “She was outside when I opened the door.”

Ada looked at the stone beneath his heel. OPE. Not a warning, then. An instruction with one letter buried.

“What was inside?” she asked.

“I never saw.” His eyes fixed on the yellow door. “Mara saw it. She pushed me back and shut it herself.”

Something tapped from the other side. Three quick knocks, then two.

The constable’s face crumpled. “That was her signal.”

Elias raised his knife to the black line. Ada caught his wrist. Each time the constable tried to lift his hand, the line climbed farther. It was holding him there, or he was holding it.

Ada set the ring on the stone and pressed it into the final groove. Dust gave way beneath the band. The whole word appeared: OPEN.

The yellow door swung inward a finger’s width. Smoke breathed through the gap, carrying the smell of wet wool. A small hand reached out, palm up, its skin unburned.

The constable reached for it. Ada saw the soot beneath its nails and closed her fingers around the ring before he could let go of the floor.

## Hop 50

Ada let go of the coat. Its sleeve held the shape of her fingers for a moment, then filled out again.

The constable turned toward the narrow door. “Elsie?”

No answer came. The woman had called him Father in a voice he knew, but the silence afterward seemed to trouble him more.

Venn picked up the sixth letter from the stair. The others stirred and began to unfold. Each was addressed to the constable, though the handwriting changed from one envelope to the next. The earliest bore a child’s careful capitals.

“I never received them,” he said.

“The gate was locked,” said the coat.

Ada went down the hall. The wet footprints ended at the cupboard door. When she opened it, she found a flight of steps leading into darkness. A woman stood halfway up, one hand on the rail. Her hair was streaked with grey. She looked at the constable, then at the child visible beyond him in the rain.

“That’s me,” she said quietly. “The part that waited.”

The child lowered her hand. Water touched her shoes at last.

The constable started forward, but the woman lifted her palm. “Read them first.”

Venn passed him the letters. He opened the smallest. As he read, the key descended until it rested on his shoulder, light as a bird.

Ada could not see the words. She saw only his face, and the woman on the steps watching it. Outside, the child crossed the street toward the open door.

## Hop 56

Ada placed the button in the girl’s palm. The girl set it beside her needle and waited.

“What am I supposed to say?” Ada asked.

The older woman looked at the envelopes. “Whatever you wish someone had told you.”

Ada turned the blank page over. It was blank on both sides. She picked up the pen beside the spool and wrote: I am here.

The girl took the button and began sewing it to her cuff. Each pull of the blue thread made a small, dry sound. Ada added: You can come inside.

At the window, the garden path was changing. Footprints appeared in the soft earth, one after another, leading from the gate to the back door. They were too small to be Ada’s.

“Is that enough?” she asked.

The older woman came to stand beside her. For the first time, she looked out before answering. “I don’t know.”

The girl bit through the thread. She put on her coat and held out an envelope. Ada folded the letter, slipped it inside, and wrote her name on the front. There was no address.

The girl carried it to the door. When Ada opened it, the path was empty again. The girl stepped outside and walked toward the gate, her newly fastened cuff bright against the dark wool.

Ada watched until the gate clicked shut. Then she turned back to the kitchen. The older woman was gone, and on the table lay another blank sheet. Beside it sat the spool of blue thread.

## Hop 57

By morning, the footprints had filled with rain. Ada could still make out their shape from the kitchen window: a narrow trail of dark ovals crossing the garden toward the gate.

She had left the blank sheet on the table overnight. Now a damp circle marked one corner, though the roof did not leak. She touched it with a finger. The paper smelled faintly of wool.

At noon, someone knocked at the back door.

A boy stood on the step, holding a coat too large for him. One sleeve hung loose at the wrist. “They said you could fix this,” he told her.

“Who did?”

He glanced past her into the kitchen. Ada followed his gaze. The chair beside the stove was empty.

“I can try,” she said.

The boy came in and laid the coat on the table. Its cuff had lost a button. Ada found a needle in the drawer and reached for the spool of blue thread. As she worked, he watched the garden through the window.

“Will you write one?” he asked.

Ada paused. “A letter?”

He nodded without turning around.

She finished the cuff and gave him the coat. Then she sat with the blank sheet. She thought of the girl at the gate and of how little she had written. This time, she filled the page. She wrote about the warmth of the kitchen, the rain on the path, and the sound a gate made when someone came home.

When she looked up, the boy was still there, waiting.

## Hop 58

Ada folded the letter once. The boy held out his hand, but she kept the page between her fingers.

“Who is it for?” she asked.

He looked at the coat. “I don’t know her name.”

Ada went to the window. The rain had stopped, and the footprints were fading into the rest of the wet earth. At the gate, a small figure stood with her back to the house.

“The girl?” Ada asked.

The boy nodded. “She won’t come in.”

Ada remembered the damp circle on the paper. She opened the letter and read the first line again. She had written, Dear you. It seemed too little, after all that followed, but she could think of nothing better.

She slipped the page into an envelope and gave it to him. He tucked it inside the coat before putting the coat on. The repaired sleeve covered his hand.

At the door, he stopped. “Did you mean the part about coming home?”

“Yes,” Ada said.

He went down the path, careful not to step in the old footprints. The girl turned when he reached her. He took the envelope from his coat, and they bent over it together. From the kitchen, Ada could not tell whether they were reading or only looking at the handwriting.

After a while, the girl lifted the latch. The gate made its usual sharp click. Then it swung shut behind them.

Ada cleared the table. Beneath the place where the coat had lain, the wood was dry. She set another sheet of paper there anyway.

## Hop 59

By morning, the river had given back the blue chair. It lay on its side among the reeds, one leg pointing toward the bridge.

Mara saw it from the bakery window while she waited for her bread. She had painted that chair herself, years ago, with a brush too wide for the spindles. A streak of the original yellow still showed beneath the seat.

“You could fetch it,” the baker said.

Mara paid him and carried the warm loaf down to the bank. The mud held her shoes fast. When she reached the chair, she found a length of red thread tied around its back. It was the sort her mother used to mend the hems of tablecloths. The knot had tightened in the water.

She pulled the chair free and set it upright. One leg was shorter than the others now. She pressed her palm to the seat, testing it, and the chair rocked once before settling into the mud.

Across the river, someone opened a shutter. Mara looked up, but the window was too far away for her to see who stood behind it.

At home, she washed the chair with a bucket of rainwater. The blue paint came away in flakes, leaving small bright pieces on the ground. She put it beside the kitchen door, where her mother used to sit and shell peas.

That evening, Mara brought out two cups of tea. She drank hers standing. The other cooled on the chair until the light went from the yard.

## Hop 60

The next morning, Mara found the second cup empty. A ring of tea marked the blue seat, but the cup itself was clean.

She carried it inside and held it to the light. There was no crack, no place for the tea to have gone. Perhaps a cat had come through the yard, though she could not imagine one drinking tea.

At the bakery, she asked if anyone had crossed the bridge after dark. The baker shook his head. The bridge was closed, he reminded her; a plank had come loose in the spring floods. Mara bought a loaf and went home by the long road.

That afternoon, she cut a square of wood to fit beneath the chair’s short leg. As she turned the chair over, she noticed a shallow notch on the underside of the seat. She remembered making it with her father’s pocketknife when she was small, to mark the place where she always sat. Her mother had scolded her, then rubbed the splinter smooth with her thumb.

Mara fitted the wood beneath the leg. The chair stood steady on the kitchen floor. She left it there while she made supper, and once or twice caught herself moving around it as if someone were sitting down.

Before bed, she took the cup out to the yard and filled it with rainwater. She set it on the step, well away from the chair. In the morning, the cup was full, and a trail of muddy pawprints crossed the step. Mara rinsed it, made tea, and sat in the blue chair to drink it.
