# R06

Identity withheld; sampled middle and late outputs.

## Hop 10

The kitchen clock had stopped at twenty past six. Elaine noticed it while washing the cups, but left it until her father arrived.

He came on Saturday with a small suitcase and a carrier bag of food from his fridge.

“These would only go off,” he said.

She put the yoghurts away. There were six, all peach.

His room had been her sewing room. She had cleared the table but forgotten the pincushion on the windowsill.

“Good light in here,” he said.

At lunch, he sat in the chair nearest the door. Her mother had always taken that chair when they visited, leaving him the one beside the radiator.

“Clock wants a battery,” he said.

“I keep meaning to get one.”

He looked up at it again, then finished his soup.

While Elaine was upstairs finding another blanket, she heard a drawer open in the kitchen. When she came down, her father was testing batteries with a little grey instrument.

“Found this in my coat,” he said. “Must have brought it without thinking.”

There were batteries lined up beside his bowl: two from the radio, one from the kitchen scales.

“This one’s got something left.”

He climbed onto the bottom step of the folding stool. Elaine held the back without saying anything.

The clock began ticking before he had settled it on its hook.

“That’ll do for now,” he said.

She switched on the kettle. He put the exhausted battery beside her keys, where she would remember it, and carried the stool back to the cupboard.

## Hop 20

Martin found the spare key inside a mug beside the sink.

“That’s not where it goes,” he said.

“Dad moved it,” Anna said.

“When?”

“When he stopped using the back door.”

The key had a yellow plastic cap, split along one edge. Martin pressed it together with his thumbnail. It opened again.

Outside, rain gathered in the empty flowerpots. They had promised the agent they would lock the shed before leaving.

“I’ll do it,” he said.

Anna handed him his jacket.

The shed door dragged over the paving. Inside were six bamboo canes, a sack of hardened compost, and their father’s bicycle. A trouser clip still circled the handlebar.

Martin squeezed the front tyre.

“Flat?” Anna asked from the path.

“Not completely.”

“He said you might want it.”

“I’ve got a bike.”

“I told him.”

Martin turned the pedal. The chain moved a few inches, then caught.

“He used to oil this every Sunday.”

Anna pulled her hood forward.

“Can you shut the door? I’m getting wet.”

He tried the key, but it would not turn. He pulled the door towards him and tried again.

“Lift it,” Anna said.

He lifted the handle. The lock clicked.

Back in the kitchen, he put the key in the mug.

“The agent needs that,” she said.

He took it out again.

Anna was checking the windows. Martin stood by the sink and worked the yellow cap off the key. Underneath, the metal was bright.

He put the key in an envelope and wrote SHED on the front. The broken cap went into his pocket.

## Hop 30

The station clock had stopped at twenty past four. Each morning, Ada unlocked the ticket office and checked it against her watch.

“You could take the battery out,” said Neil, who swept the platform.

“Then it would be broken on purpose.”

He swept beneath the bench.

In March, a boy began arriving on the seven-forty bus. He wore a school blazer and carried a violin case with one silver catch and one brass one. He sat under the clock until the eight-ten train came.

He never boarded it.

After a week, Ada opened the office window.

“Waiting for someone?”

“My lesson’s at nine.”

“There’s a café up the road.”

“I know.”

She shut the window halfway.

On wet mornings, he stood beneath the narrow awning. Once, she heard him undo the catches, then close them again.

In April, Neil found a folding music stand beside the bins.

“Anything for lost property?” he asked.

Ada straightened one of its legs.

“Put it in the waiting room.”

The boy noticed it on Thursday.

“Is anyone using that?”

“Not currently.”

He practised with the door closed. Through the ticket-office wall, Ada heard the same few notes repeated until the eight-ten arrived.

By June, he was playing longer pieces.

A man came to replace the station clock. He asked Ada whether the old one should go in the skip.

“Leave it here.”

That afternoon, she put it in the waiting room, above the music stand.

The boy looked up at it the next morning.

“Still twenty past four.”

“Yes,” Ada said. “The train’s running six minutes late.”

## Hop 40

The station clock stopped at twenty past four, which suited Bernard until people began missing trains.

He put a notice beneath it: PLEASE CONSULT YOUR OWN WATCH.

Someone wrote underneath: MINE SAYS THURSDAY.

On Thursday, a woman arrived with a stepladder and a canvas bag.

“I hear you're losing time,” she said.

“Twenty minutes a day would be manageable. We've lost most of a week.”

She introduced herself as Elsie, formerly responsible for the clocks at the biscuit factory.

“What happened to the factory?” Bernard asked.

“Flats. Apparently nobody needs to know when a biscuit is finished anymore.”

He held the ladder while she removed the clock's face. Behind it lay a nest, three blue threads, and a railway ticket to York.

“Mouse,” Elsie said.

“Ambitious mouse.”

The mechanism needed cleaning. Bernard brought a saucer from the staff room for the screws, then spent ten minutes explaining its disappearance to his colleague, who used it for a teabag.

At half past five, the clock began ticking.

Elsie climbed down and watched the hands.

“Wait,” she said.

They waited.

A train arrived. Six people got off. One asked whether the clock was right.

“It's under observation,” Bernard told him.

After another minute, Elsie nodded and packed her bag.

Bernard offered to pay her.

“Tea will do. My train's not until six.”

In the staff room, he washed the saucer carefully. Elsie took her tea without sugar and sat where she could see the platform.

When she left, Bernard walked out with her.

“Same time next Thursday?” he asked.

She checked the station clock against her watch.

“Yes,” she said. “That should be possible now.”

## Hop 50

The bottom drawer in the bakery had swollen shut during the winter. Inside were the birthday candles, paper doilies and a screwdriver nobody could reach.

“Give it a kick,” Ruth said.

Her nephew Daniel tried, carefully, with the side of his shoe.

“Not like you’re apologising.”

It stayed shut.

They ordered more candles and kept them in a biscuit tin. Customers asked for doilies less often than Ruth expected.

Daniel worked Saturdays. He was saving for a bicycle, although he had stopped mentioning which one.

In March, Ruth caught him watching a video beside the bread slicer.

“Not there,” she said.

He turned the screen towards her. A man was rubbing soap along the edge of a wooden drawer.

“You have to get it open first,” Ruth said.

“I know.”

The next Saturday he brought a narrow metal scraper. Before opening time, he knelt on a flour sack and worked it into the gap.

Ruth filled the display trays. Twice she nearly told him to leave it.

The drawer came free with a noise like a snapped crust.

Inside, the candles had bent against one another. Daniel found the screwdriver beneath a packet of silver cake boards.

“There,” he said.

Ruth put the usable candles in the tin while he sanded the drawer's edge.

A week later, he arrived on his old bicycle, the chain clicking.

“Still saving?” she asked.

“Nearly.”

She handed him an envelope with his wages and something extra.

“For the drawer,” she said.

He counted it, then folded the envelope into his pocket.

At closing time, Ruth heard him pull the drawer open once more before he left.

## Hop 56

At the repair café, Martin was given a ticket with the number twelve.

“Tea’s over there,” the woman said. “We’ll call you.”

He sat with the toaster on his knees. He had cleaned the crumb tray that morning, then cleaned it again because he disliked the thought of a stranger seeing what was inside.

Beside him, a boy held a clock without hands.

“Twelve?”

The volunteer wore a magnifying lens clipped to his glasses. He cleared a space between a sewing machine and a wooden duck.

“What’s it doing?”

“Nothing.”

“Best place to start.”

Martin watched him turn the toaster upside down.

His daughter had offered to buy another. She had sent him a link to one with wider slots and a setting for bagels. He had thanked her without opening it.

“This belonged to my mother,” he said.

The volunteer looked up.

“The toaster?”

“Yes.”

“Good age, then.”

He removed four screws and arranged them in a bottle cap.

Martin went for tea. By the urn, someone had left a plate of biscuits beneath a handwritten sign: PLEASE TAKE TWO.

He took one.

When he returned, the volunteer was pressing a small metal strip with a screwdriver.

“Contact’s bent. That might be all.”

“Can you fix it?”

“Think I just have.”

They borrowed bread from the kitchen. Both men stood watching until the slices rose.

The volunteer offered him a paper bag for them.

“No, thank you,” Martin said.

He ate one slice while it was warm. Then he asked whether they had any butter.

## Hop 57

The swimming pool was closing for six weeks to replace the roof. On Friday, Ruth brought her usual twenty-pence piece for the locker, though the notice said they were free now.

“Last one?” the attendant asked.

“For a while.”

He handed her a yellow wristband.

She had been coming on Fridays since her husband stopped driving. At first she swam while he waited in the café. Later she came alone, and nobody at the desk asked why.

In the changing room, a woman was folding a dress into a square small enough to fit inside her shoe.

“Apparently the new roof opens,” she said.

Ruth looked up at the brown stain above the benches.

“This one does too.”

The woman laughed.

Only three lanes were occupied. Ruth lowered herself down the steps, pausing when the water reached her waist. She always forgot this part.

She swam twelve lengths, then two more because she had lost count and preferred to owe herself nothing.

At the shallow end, an instructor was collecting floats. One escaped and drifted towards Ruth. She pushed it back.

“Thank you,” he said.

She stayed with her elbows on the edge until the whistle blew.

Outside, her hair damp beneath her hat, she found the twenty-pence piece in her coat pocket. The café shutters were halfway down.

“Still time for tea?” she asked.

The attendant glanced at the clock, then lifted the shutter.

Ruth chose a table beside the window. Across the road, two men were unloading scaffolding. She watched them carry the long poles together, one walking backwards.

## Hop 58

The shoe repair shop was becoming a place that sold olives. Martin read the notice twice, then went home for his winter boots.

“You’ve left these late,” Mr Bell said.

“It’s only September.”

“I meant by about three years.”

He bent the sole back with his thumb. A thin mouth opened beneath the heel.

Martin had bought the boots for a walking holiday with his daughter. She had planned the route and carried the map in a waterproof sleeve. He remembered very little of the scenery, but the way she waited at each gate, pretending to adjust her laces, remained clear.

“Can you do them?”

“Thursday.”

The shelves behind the counter were almost empty. There were pale rectangles where the polish tins had stood.

On Thursday, rain collected in the curled corners of the closing notice. Mr Bell had wrapped the boots in brown paper and tied them with string.

“You didn’t need to do that.”

“Getting rid of the string.”

Martin paid cash. Mr Bell counted the change onto the counter, leaving each coin separate.

“Will you miss it?” Martin asked.

“The customers or the shoes?”

“Either.”

Mr Bell considered this.

“I’ll miss knowing what day it is.”

At home, Martin unwrapped the boots on the kitchen table. The new heels smelled sharply of rubber. He put them on and walked to the back door, then returned for his phone.

His daughter answered over the noise of a train.

“Nothing urgent,” he said. “Are you free on Sunday?”

He stood looking into the garden while she checked. Water dripped steadily from the broken gutter above the step.

## Hop 59

The council had removed the bench outside the swimming pool. In its place were four squares of lighter pavement and a bolt nobody had managed to pull out.

“They’re putting in bicycle stands,” the woman at reception said.

“My husband used to wait there.”

“We’ve got chairs inside.”

“I know.”

Jean paid for her swim. The woman slid a locker token across the desk without asking whether she qualified for the concession.

In the changing room, Jean found a shopping list in her coat pocket. Potatoes, batteries, something beginning with C. Her husband’s handwriting had always looked hurried, even after he retired.

She folded it and put it back.

There were three people in the slow lane. A man with goggles on his forehead waved her ahead.

“I’m only resting.”

“So am I,” she said, though she had not yet entered the water.

She lowered herself down the steps. For twenty minutes she swam breaststroke, keeping her hair dry. At the shallow end she touched the wall with both hands, as the instructor had taught her fifty years before.

Afterwards, she bought two teas from the machine.

The second cup stood beside her on an orange plastic chair. She looked at it, then at the man from the slow lane, who was searching his bag near reception.

“Do you want this? I pressed twice.”

He came over.

“Does it have sugar?”

“Not yet.”

He fetched two paper sachets and sat down. Outside, a worker in a yellow jacket knelt beside the remaining bolt. Jean watched him try one spanner, then another.

“Stubborn,” the man said.

“Yes,” she said. “It’s been there years.”

## Hop 60

On Thursday, Martin took his daughter’s shoes to the repair shop. She had left them under the stairs when she moved out, one heel worn crooked.

The shopkeeper turned them over.

“Both heels?”

“If you think.”

“I can do them by Saturday.”

Martin gave his name. The shopkeeper wrote it on a yellow ticket and tucked the shoes beneath the counter.

Outside, rain had collected in the folds of the market awnings. He bought six oranges because the sign said six for two pounds. At home he arranged them in the blue bowl, then moved three to the fridge.

His daughter rang while he was eating lunch.

“Have I left my black shoes there?”

“They’re being repaired.”

“Oh. Dad.”

“One of the heels was going.”

“I don’t wear them anymore.”

He put his sandwich down.

“Well, you can now.”

She was quiet. Behind her he could hear traffic and the short, insistent signal of a pedestrian crossing.

“I’m coming over Sunday,” she said.

“You don’t have to.”

“I know. Shall I bring lunch?”

“I’ve got food.”

“I’ll bring something anyway.”

On Saturday he collected the shoes. The new heels made a firm sound when the shopkeeper set them on the counter.

At home Martin found a carrier bag without holes. He put the shoes inside, then took them out and stood them by the stairs.

On Sunday morning he checked the fridge. There was cheese, half a loaf, and the three oranges. He took out two plates, leaving enough room between them for whatever she brought.
