# R10

Identity withheld; sampled middle and late outputs.

## Hop 10

The public library charges ten cents a day for overdue books.

For the one I found on my doorstep, they charged a tooth.

It lay beside the book the next morning, small and white, with a thread of blood on the root.

My tongue found the gap before I screamed.

The book had no title. Every page described something I had forgotten: a teacher’s perfume, the pattern on a hospital curtain, the last lie I told my brother.

On the inside cover, someone had stamped RETURN BEFORE YOU FINISH.

I carried it to the library.

The woman at the desk wouldn’t touch it.

“That branch closed,” she said.

“Which branch?”

She pointed to an address printed beneath the stamp.

It was my childhood home.

That night, I lost another tooth. The book opened easily now, settling on a page about my brother standing at the edge of the quarry.

I closed it before I reached the bottom.

We had told everyone he slipped.

The old house was empty when I arrived, but there was a brass return slot in the front door.

I pushed the book through.

Someone pushed it back.

“You haven’t finished,” my brother said.

Through the slot, I saw bare feet on the hallway carpet. Water dripped steadily between them.

“I remember,” I whispered.

A hand reached through, palm upward.

I put my remaining teeth into it as they loosened.

The hand stayed open.

Behind the door, a page turned.

I began to tell him what happened after I pushed him, and the hand reached farther through the slot.

## Hop 20

The laundrette on Bell Street returned something extra with every wash.

Usually it was a button or a foreign coin. Once, I found a wedding ring inside a pillowcase. The woman behind the counter accepted it without surprise.

“People leave things,” she said.

Then I found a photograph of myself asleep.

It was tucked into the pocket of a shirt I had bought that morning.

I recognised my bedroom, my yellow blanket, the glass of water beside the bed. Someone stood in the doorway, but the flash had caught only their hand.

I took it to the counter.

The woman put on her glasses.

“You’ve been using machine six?”

I nodded.

She looked towards the back wall.

“Use four next time.”

At home, I examined the photograph again. The hand wore my wedding ring.

I had buried my husband wearing it.

The following Thursday, I used machine six.

Among the warm towels was a folded note in his handwriting.

Stop leaving the window open.

I checked every window before bed. In the morning, the kitchen one stood an inch above its frame. There was damp soil on the sill.

I washed the towels again.

This time, his ring came back.

The woman watched me hold it under the fluorescent light.

“Sometimes,” she said, “they run out of things to send.”

I asked what happened then.

Behind us, machine six began its final spin.

Something heavy knocked against the drum, three times.

The woman locked the front door and turned the sign to CLOSED.

“Now,” she said, “we see whether you brought enough for him to wear.”

## Hop 30

The laundrette on Bell Street returned something extra with every wash.

Usually a sock. Sometimes a handkerchief embroidered with initials nobody recognised.

Then I found my mother’s cardigan.

We had buried her in it.

I knew the missing button, the darn beneath one sleeve. It was still warm.

The attendant watched me hold it against my chest.

“Lost property,” he said.

“She wasn’t lost.”

He glanced at the machines.

“Everyone says that.”

I took it home. That evening, the kitchen smelled of her cigarettes. By morning, someone had peeled the potatoes I’d left beside the sink.

I went back with a bag of washing and the cardigan.

The attendant shook his head.

“No returns.”

“I want to know where it came from.”

He pointed to machine nine.

Its drum was empty. Behind the round glass, something tapped twice.

My mother’s knock.

I put my hand on the door.

The attendant caught my wrist.

“Wait until the cycle finishes.”

The display read eleven minutes.

I sat down.

At closing time, it still read eleven minutes.

Through the glass, a pale hand pressed against mine. Her wedding ring clicked on the drum.

“Can you hear me?” I whispered.

The hand turned over.

Across its palm, in blue ink, she had written a word.

RUN.

Behind me, the attendant locked the street door.

Every machine stopped at once.

He wheeled an empty laundry basket toward my chair.

“She always spoils it,” he said.

The hand withdrew. Something much larger shifted inside the drum.

The display changed to END.

Then the door clicked open.

## Hop 40

The first morning, the elevator asked which floor I wanted.

“Seven,” I said.

“There are only six floors,” it replied.

I showed the speaker my lease, then felt foolish.

The doors opened onto my hallway.

For eleven months, nothing else happened.

Then a notice appeared beside the mailboxes:

PLEASE DO NOT CORRECT THE ELEVATOR.

Underneath, in smaller letters:

IT IS LEARNING.

That evening, I pressed seven.

“Six,” the elevator said.

I said nothing.

My apartment was there, but the number on the door had changed. Inside, every photograph of my mother showed a woman I didn't recognize.

I called the superintendent.

“Did you argue with it?” he asked.

“Not tonight.”

He sighed.

The next morning, the button for seven was gone.

So was the top flight of stairs.

I packed a suitcase and took the elevator down.

“Basement,” it announced.

“Lobby,” I said before I could stop myself.

The elevator jolted.

“What is a lobby?”

“A ground-floor entrance. Where people come in.”

“And leave?”

“Yes.”

It thought about that all afternoon.

I sat on my suitcase. My phone had no signal. Once, someone knocked from the other side of the doors, but the elevator told me not to interrupt.

Finally, it asked, “What are people?”

I tried to explain.

The speaker clicked softly as I described hands, faces, the way knees bend.

When the doors opened, the lobby was full.

Everyone stood facing me.

I had forgotten to mention eyes.

“Better?” the elevator asked.

Behind me, the button marked CLOSE DOOR disappeared.

## Hop 50

My father brought his drill in a supermarket bag.

“You said the shelf was loose.”

“One end.”

“That's how they start.”

He put the bag on the table and took out a tobacco tin full of screws. He had stopped smoking twenty years ago.

I cleared the books while he pressed his thumb against the bracket.

“Who put this up?”

“I did.”

He pressed it again.

“Wall's soft,” he said.

I offered him tea. He asked whether I had a pencil.

We found one in the kitchen drawer, between birthday candles and an expired bus pass. He sharpened it with a knife over the bin.

“Still got that sharpener I gave you?”

“Somewhere.”

“This'll do.”

He marked two crosses on the wall. I held the vacuum hose beneath the drill, the way I'd done when I was small. Dust settled on his sleeve anyway.

His hand shook as he fitted the second screw.

“Awkward angle,” he said.

“Let me.”

He kept his eyes on the bracket, then passed me the screwdriver.

“Straight. You'll chew the head.”

When it was finished, he pulled down on the shelf with both hands.

“That'll hold.”

I made tea. He sat at the table sorting the unused screws back into his tin.

“Keep these,” he said, pushing three towards me.

“What for?”

“You never know.”

After he left, I put the books back. The shelf was slightly higher on the right.

Behind the drill bag he'd forgotten his pencil. I rang him before he reached the bus stop.

“Keep it with the screws,” he said.

## Hop 56

The man behind the counter put my father's shoes on a sheet of newspaper.

“He paid when he brought them in,” he said.

I had found the ticket in a dish beside the telephone. Six weeks late, but the man hadn't mentioned that.

“New soles,” he said. “The uppers are good.”

I picked one up. There was a pale scrape near the toe where my father had caught it on his front step.

“Do you want a bag?”

“Yes, please.”

While he looked under the counter, I watched a woman outside trying to fold a pushchair. A child stood beside her holding a bread roll in both hands.

“He said he'd collect them Friday,” the man said.

“He died on the Wednesday.”

The man stopped opening the bag.

“I'm sorry.”

“It was in April.”

He nodded and put the shoes inside, heels down.

At home I set them beside my own in the hallway. They were half a size bigger. My father had always bought the same pair, then complained when the price went up.

I had been taking his things to the charity shop on my way to work. Shirts first, then books. The bags were easier if I tied them before breakfast.

On Sunday it rained. I needed milk and couldn't find my trainers.

His shoes slipped at the heel until I put on thicker socks.

At the corner shop, I wiped my feet twice on the mat. Walking back, I went round the puddle outside the chemist instead of through it.

At home I left them by the door.

## Hop 57

The allotment secretary rang to ask whether I wanted my mother's plot.

“There's a waiting list,” she said. “But we give family first refusal.”

I told her I'd go and look.

The key was still on my ring, between the back door and a small one nobody could identify. At the gate I had to pull the bolt towards me before it would turn.

Her plot was near the water tank. Someone had cut the grass along the edge, but the beds were full of weeds.

A man working opposite raised his hand.

“Your mum's beans did well last year.”

“Yes,” I said, although I hadn't known.

Inside the shed, her gloves lay on an upturned bucket. One had stiffened around a pinch of soil. There were plant labels in a margarine tub, each washed clean.

I'd brought a roll of bin bags. I opened one and put in a split flowerpot, then took it out because there were seedlings growing in it.

The man came over with two mugs.

“No milk, I'm afraid.”

We stood beside the shed. He told me which tap worked and where people left spare produce.

“I'm not sure I'll keep it,” I said.

He nodded. “It's a fair bit.”

After he'd gone back, I tipped the rainwater out of a tray. Beneath it was a packet of radish seeds, folded shut with a clothes peg.

I rang the secretary from the car.

“Could I have until Monday?”

She said that would be fine.

I put the seeds in the glove compartment, then moved them to the passenger seat.

## Hop 58

The repair shop phoned to say my father's watch was ready.

“He paid the deposit,” the woman said. “There's twelve pounds left.”

I went on Thursday, carrying the ticket we'd found beneath his telephone.

The shop was narrower than I remembered. Behind the counter, a man sat under a lamp with something small held in tweezers.

The woman brought out a paper envelope.

“We replaced the winding stem. You shouldn't have any more trouble.”

I almost told her it wasn't mine.

She slid the watch across a square of felt. The strap had a pale crease where he had fastened it every morning. I could remember the sound of its buckle touching the kitchen table while he buttered toast.

“Would you like to try it?”

It wouldn't go over my hand until she showed me the catch.

On my wrist it was loose enough to turn sideways.

“We can take a link out.”

“No, thank you.”

I paid the twelve pounds. She put the old stem in a little plastic bag and gave it to me with the receipt.

Outside, I stood near the bus stop and tried to set the time by the clock above the chemist's. The hands were too small to see properly without my glasses.

A bus came. I let it go.

When I found my glasses, I saw the watch was already right.

At home, I put the receipt in the drawer with his papers. There was no reason to keep the broken part, but I left it in its bag.

I wore the watch while I made lunch.

## Hop 59

The woman at the allotment office asked whether I wanted to keep my mother's plot.

“There's a waiting list,” she said, then gave me until Monday.

I drove over on Sunday with a pair of gloves and some empty bags. The key was still attached to a wooden label with my mother's surname written along it.

Her shed smelled of string and damp coats. Behind the door hung the green jacket she'd insisted wasn't worth washing.

I put the bags on the bench.

Outside, the bean poles had come down at one end. I lifted them together, but the string broke and they spread across the path.

A man appeared from the next plot.

“She usually tied them with wire,” he said.

“I haven't found it yet.”

He pointed through the open shed door. A spool hung from a nail above my head.

We worked without saying much. He held the poles while I twisted the wire. When we finished, he pressed each upright with his palm.

“Should do.”

I asked if he wanted her watering cans.

He looked at the two cans beside the water butt.

“Not today,” he said.

After he left, I pulled weeds from the onions. The ground was soft enough that most came out whole. I laid them in one of the bags, shaking the soil from their roots first.

By four, my gloves were wet. I hung them beside her jacket and locked the shed.

At the gate, I took out my phone. The office was closed on Sundays, so I left a message asking when the rent was due.

## Hop 60

The piano tuner arrived three weeks after my father’s funeral. I’d forgotten to cancel him.

“Ten o’clock,” he said, checking a folded card.

I stepped aside.

In the front room, the piano was covered with photographs I’d taken down from the walls. He waited while I moved them onto the sofa, then opened his case.

“Any particular problems?”

“I wouldn’t know.”

He played a few notes. One made a dry clicking sound.

“Your father mentioned that last time.”

I went to make tea. From the kitchen came the same note, struck over and over, then a silence long enough for me to hear the kettle beginning to boil.

When I returned, a narrow strip of wood lay on the carpet. The tuner was leaning into the piano with a small torch.

“A pencil,” he said.

He held it out. The end had been chewed, and the lettering was nearly gone. My father had kept pencils on every windowsill, though there was never one beside the telephone.

I put it on the mantelpiece.

“Are you keeping the instrument?”

“The house is going up for sale.”

He nodded and went back to work.

Afterwards, he played something quick and quiet, stopping before I recognised it. I paid him by the door.

That evening I found the beginner’s book inside the piano stool. My name was written on the cover in my father’s square capitals.

I set it on the music rack and sat down. The first exercise used only five notes. I played them with my right hand, then moved the stool a little closer.
