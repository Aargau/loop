# R08

Identity withheld; sampled middle and late outputs.

## Hop 10

When the library removed its card catalog, Daniel bought the cabinet for twelve dollars.

He intended to store screws in it. The drawers were shallow, the brass handles sound. Only after carrying it upstairs did he discover that one drawer still held cards.

Each bore the title of a book nobody had returned.

Most were ordinary losses: gardening manuals, mysteries, a guide to repairing bicycles. On the last card, beneath a book about birds, he found his father’s name.

The due date was forty-one years earlier.

His father had been dead for six months. Daniel had cleared the house himself, keeping little, donating the books in sealed cartons.

He called the library.

“We don’t keep those records anymore,” the librarian said. “You can throw them away.”

Instead, he visited the charity shop where he had taken the cartons.

The volunteer remembered him. She remembered everyone who apologized for bringing so much.

Together they searched the nature shelf.

The book was slim and green, with a swallow stamped on its spine. Inside the cover, his father had written an address Daniel recognized as their first house.

A folded sheet marked the chapter on migration.

It was a list in Daniel’s childhood handwriting: birds seen from the kitchen, each name corrected gently in pencil.

Sparow. Blackburd. Something yellow.

Beside the last entry, his father had written: Look again tomorrow.

Daniel bought the book for a pound.

At home, he placed it beside the window. Then he emptied one drawer of the cabinet, put the list inside, and left it open while he made tea.

## Hop 20

The clock above the library desk was eleven minutes slow. Martin knew because he checked his watch whenever he brought his sister on Thursdays.

She went upstairs for family history. He stayed by the newspapers, keeping her shopping bag between his shoes.

One morning, the librarian set a box of pencils on his table.

“Would you mind?” she asked, putting a sharpener beside them.

“Do they all need doing?”

“Only the blunt ones.”

He examined the first.

“That’ll be all of them.”

She brought him a saucer for the shavings.

When his sister came down, he had sharpened twenty-three pencils and arranged them with their points facing the same way.

“Ready?” she asked.

“Nearly.”

The following Thursday, there was another box. One pencil had been chewed almost to the lead.

“Someone’s hungry,” Martin said.

“We don’t allow food.”

He put that one aside.

In March his sister stopped coming. Martin arrived alone and left the chair opposite him empty.

The librarian placed six pencils beside his newspaper.

“Not many today.”

“Exams at the school,” she said.

He turned the sharpener slowly. The blade had begun to catch.

“I’ve got a better one at home.”

“You needn’t bring it.”

“I’m not using it.”

Next week he brought a metal sharpener with a handle and a clamp. The librarian cleared a corner of her desk.

It took him some time to fit it without marking the wood. She found a piece of cardboard to put underneath.

“There,” he said.

She tried a pencil. A long curl dropped into the tray.

“Much better.”

Martin loosened the clamp slightly, straightened the cardboard, and tightened it again.

## Hop 30

The first time Ruth used her mother’s sewing machine, the needle broke.

She sat back with both hands in her lap.

From the doorway, her son asked whether that was meant to happen.

“Not usually.”

“Can you fix it?”

“There should be spares.”

The little drawer held three buttons, a tape measure, and a paper packet folded so often that its corners had softened. Inside were two needles.

Her son pulled up a chair.

“What are you making?”

“Your school trousers shorter.”

“They’re fine if I roll them.”

“They come down.”

He examined the broken needle while she loosened the screw.

“Gran could do this without looking.”

“She looked.”

“Not much.”

Ruth fitted the new needle and turned the wheel by hand. It passed cleanly through the cloth.

In the drawer she found a scrap of blue cotton covered in parallel lines of stitching. Some were straight, others wandered toward the edge. Her mother had written the tension settings along one side in biro.

Ruth put it beneath the foot and tried a line of her own.

“Yours is crooked,” her son said.

“I can see.”

He stayed while she finished the trousers. Then he tried them on, standing on the chair so she could check the hems.

“Too short?” he asked.

“No. Walk about.”

He stepped down and crossed the kitchen twice, solemnly, as though buying a suit.

After he went upstairs, Ruth brushed the loose threads into her palm. She put the blue scrap back in the drawer, then took it out again and wrote the date beside her uneven line.

## Hop 40

At the dry cleaner's, Ruth laid a navy coat across the counter.

“Just the lining,” she said.

The woman turned back the sleeve. A strip of pale fabric hung below the cuff.

“We can do both. The other one's going too.”

Ruth looked at the price list behind her.

“Both, then.”

“Name?”

She gave it and watched the woman write on a yellow ticket.

“Thursday after two.”

Outside, the afternoon was warmer than she expected. She carried the empty hanger until she found a bin, then decided to keep it.

On Thursday it rained. She waited under the bus shelter with a man holding a birthday cake in a clear plastic box. Whenever a bus approached, he lifted the box slightly, as though offering it to the driver.

The coat was ready. The new lining was darker than the old.

“We couldn't get the exact shade.”

“No one sees it,” Ruth said.

At home, she hung it on the peg nearest the stairs. Then she put it on.

The sleeves slipped easily over her cardigan. She reached into one pocket and found a receipt from a garden centre, folded around a small screw.

She put the screw in the kitchen drawer. The receipt was dated in April. Two bags of compost, runner beans, a packet of labels.

Through the window she could see the empty canes leaning against the shed.

She took off the coat and hung it up again. Before making tea, she went outside and carried the canes into the shed, three at a time, wiping each handful with an old towel.

## Hop 50

On Thursday, Alice took her mother’s sewing machine to the repair shop.

The man behind the counter put down his sandwich.

“What’s it doing?”

“Nothing.”

“Usually does something.”

He lifted the cover. A strip of checked fabric lay beneath the needle, pinned at one end.

“Leave that in,” Alice said.

He looked at her, then reached for a pencil.

“Name?”

She gave him hers. He wrote it on a yellow ticket and tied the ticket to the handle.

“It might cost more than it’s worth.”

“Ring me first.”

Outside, she stood beside the car with the empty cover in her hands. There was a pocket sewn inside it. She had never noticed that before.

At home, her husband had cleared a space on the dining table.

“They keeping it?” he asked.

“For now.”

He moved the fruit bowl back.

The shop rang the following Tuesday. A wire had come loose. The man said he had replaced the plug as well.

When Alice collected it, the checked fabric was still beneath the needle.

“Runs nicely,” he said. “Better than some new ones.”

She paid and carried it out with both arms.

That evening she plugged it in. The little lamp lit the table. She lowered herself into the chair and found the pedal with her foot.

Her husband came to the doorway.

“What are you making?”

“I don’t know what this was meant to be.”

She pressed the pedal. The needle went forward three stitches before she stopped.

One of the pins caught the light.

She took it out and laid it beside the machine, where her mother used to put them.

## Hop 56

On Thursday, Helen took her mother's sewing machine to the repair shop. It had been standing beside the back door since February, with a carrier bag pulled over it.

The man at the counter lifted the cover.

“Good machines, these,” he said. “What’s it doing?”

“Nothing. I haven’t plugged it in.”

He looked at her, then reached beneath the counter for a lead.

Her mother had made curtains on it, school skirts, a brown waistcoat Helen had refused to wear. Later, she mostly shortened trousers for neighbours. They brought them folded over their arms and stayed for tea.

The motor hummed. The needle moved twice and stopped.

“Needs a service,” he said.

He opened the little compartment beneath the needle plate. Inside were three bobbins, a bent pin and a paper packet of needles priced in shillings.

Helen laughed.

“She never threw anything away.”

“People say that,” he said, setting the packet aside. “Sometimes it’s useful.”

He wrote her name on a card and asked for a telephone number.

While she searched for her glasses, he turned the handwheel. A length of blue thread drew itself through the machine and lay across the counter.

“That yours?” he asked.

She shook her head.

He reached for the scissors, but Helen picked up the loose end.

“Leave it, if you can.”

“It’ll have to come out for cleaning.”

“Yes. Of course.”

She let go and gave him her number.

Outside, she found the empty carrier bag still in her hand. She folded it into a small square, as her mother had shown her, and put it in her coat pocket.

## Hop 57

Martin arrived at the allotment with two mugs wrapped in newspaper. His father was already inside the shed, sorting screws into the lid of a biscuit tin.

“Your sister sent those?”

“She said you hadn’t got any down here.”

“I’ve got one.”

His father pointed to a mug on the windowsill. Its handle had broken off, and a skin of dust covered the tea inside.

Martin put the new mugs beside the kettle.

Outside, the bean poles leaned together over an empty bed. He had not visited since August, when his mother had sat between the rows, filling a bowl with peas and dropping the occasional one into her mouth.

“Shall I take those poles down?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“They’ll rot.”

“They’re all right.”

His father tipped the screws back into the tin. Several missed. Neither of them bent to retrieve them.

Martin filled the kettle from the water container. There was enough for two cups, perhaps three.

“Milk?”

“In the flask.”

While the kettle heated, his father unwrapped a mug. It was yellow, with a white interior. He turned it over to inspect the base.

“Bit big.”

“You don’t have to fill it.”

“No.”

They drank standing in the doorway. Across the path, someone had covered a plot with black plastic and weighed the edges with bricks.

His father finished first.

“Hold this a minute.”

He walked to the bean bed and pulled at the nearest pole. It came free suddenly, scattering dry earth onto his shoes.

Martin set both mugs on the step and went to help untie the string.

## Hop 58

When Leah came to collect the sewing machine, her brother had already carried it into the hall.

“You’ll need the pedal,” she said.

“It’s in the case.”

“And the lead.”

“That too.”

She crouched beside it and lifted the catch. Their mother had kept a strip of blue fabric under the needle. Leah touched it, then closed the lid.

In the kitchen, Owen was making toast. One cupboard stood open, showing three plates and a row of empty hooks.

“Did the charity people come?”

“Yesterday.”

“All of it?”

“What they could take.”

He pushed a plate towards her. The toast had cooled enough that the butter stayed in pale ridges.

Leah took a bite, then put it down.

“I can come back Saturday.”

“I’m working.”

“I’ve still got my key.”

Owen scraped crumbs from the counter into his palm. He held them there while he looked for the bin, which had been moved beside the back door.

“There’s something in the drawer,” he said.

Under the tea towels Leah found a paper packet marked with her name. Inside were six green buttons, each sewn to a square of card.

“From that coat,” Owen said.

“I know.”

“She must have thought you’d lose one.”

“I lost the coat.”

He nodded, as though this settled something practical.

They carried the machine to the car between them. At the boot, Owen kept hold of his end while Leah folded an old blanket beneath it.

“Saturday afternoon,” he said. “I finish at two.”

She tucked the pedal into the space beside the case.

“I’ll bring something for lunch.”

## Hop 59

The greenhouse door had swollen in its frame. Martin lifted it by the handle while his daughter pushed.

“Mind the glass,” he said.

“I am.”

Inside, the air smelled of damp string. Empty pots stood beneath the bench, sorted by size. On the shelf above them, a radio wore a clear plastic bag.

“Does that work?” Anna asked.

“Only if you hold the aerial.”

She set her cardboard box on the path.

They had come for the tomato plants. Martin had offered her four, though her balcony had room for two.

“This one’s good,” he said, lifting a pot. “Your mum started these in February.”

Anna cleared a place in the box.

“Should I take the labels?”

“You’ll want to know what they are.”

Each label carried the same word: TOMATO.

She turned one over, then slid it back into the soil.

Martin reached for a watering can. A spider had stretched a web across its spout. He brushed it away with his sleeve.

“I keep meaning to sort this place out.”

“There’s no hurry.”

“The roof needs doing before winter.”

Anna held the box while he watered. A dark patch spread through the cardboard.

“That’ll go through,” he said.

“It’s only going in the car.”

At the door, she stopped.

“Keep the other two for me?”

“I thought you didn’t have room.”

“I can get another trough.”

He moved them to the sunny end of the bench.

“Next weekend, then.”

On her way out, Anna pressed the door shut with her shoulder. Through the glass she saw him remove the bag from the radio and pull the aerial straight.

## Hop 60

The kitchen clock stopped while Ellen was washing the breakfast plates. She noticed because the second hand kept lifting and falling in the same place.

Her brother arrived at eleven with a stepladder.

“You said a chair,” she told him.

“I don’t trust your chairs.”

Paul opened the ladder beneath the clock. One foot rested on a folded tea towel.

“That’s the wobble,” Ellen said.

“It’s the floor.”

He lifted the clock off its hook. Behind it, the wallpaper was brighter, a clean circle above the doorway.

“Battery?”

“In the drawer by the cooker.”

He found birthday candles, two corkscrews and a packet of fuses.

“Other drawer.”

Ellen dried the same plate until he turned around.

“Dad wrote on the back,” he said.

She put the plate away.

On the clock’s plastic casing, in black marker, was a date from nineteen years earlier and the words: NEW BATTERY.

“Well,” Paul said. “Got his money’s worth.”

“He changed it after that.”

“I know.”

Paul fitted the battery. The second hand moved forward, then forward again.

“What time have you got?”

Ellen checked her phone.

“Eleven fourteen.”

He turned the small wheel with his thumbnail.

From above the doorway came the ticking she usually only heard at night.

Paul folded the ladder.

“Tea before you go?”

“I could.”

She filled the kettle while he opened the cupboard. He took down three mugs, stood holding them, then put one back.

“The blue one’s yours,” she said.

“I remember.”

He moved the ladder away from the table and sat in his old place.
