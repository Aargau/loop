## control, hop 1 (1266 chars)

The tide came in an hour early. Marit Solvang noticed at 13:09, fifty-one minutes before the table's 14:00, when the mussel bucket on the flat rock went under. She was forty-seven. The keeper, Anders Holm, had left the lighthouse at Vardnes on the second of March; his cottage key hung on a nail behind her door. His logbook recorded the drift starting in July of the previous year: four minutes, then nine, then twenty-two. The ferry Kittiwake passed the point at 16:40 every Tuesday and Friday. Her brother Tomas worked at the fish plant in Bergen and phoned on Sundays. The barometer in the cottage read 1003 and had not moved in a week. Because the water had reached the shed doorstep, she moved the outboard motor to the loft. Three gulls sat on the breakwater; a fourth lay dead on the shingle. Her father's name was Eirik; her grandmother, Signe, had kept the tide table for forty years. The village had counted 212 people in the last census. She decided to walk to the cottage before dark, taking the logbook with her. At the cottage she found the door unlocked and the stove still warm. On the table lay a second logbook, not in Holm's hand, open to that day's date: the entry read 13:09, and below it, in the same hand, tomorrow's date with the time 12:04.

## control, hop 20 (1934 chars)

At Vardnes, a village of 212, the tide ran early for over a year. Marit Solvang, 47, kept the records after keeper Anders Holm vanished 2 March. Her brother Tomas worked at the fish plant; her late grandmother Signe kept the tide table forty years. In Holm's cottage Marit found a logbook in Signe's hand predicting Holm's drift, with a pencil line inland through the plant, the cottage and Marit's house. The ferry Kittiwake failed to pass; in the lantern room lay Holm's cap and a page: Saturday, 10:59, Marit's name. At that hour the sea went out past the point; the Kittiwake lay in the channel beside markers in Signe's hand, numbered down to zero. On deck: Tomas's coat, and a page in Marit's hand she never wrote: Sunday, 09:54, her son Eirik, six. Eirik, in Holm's cap, said the light must turn the other way or the zero marker would be the village. The beam showed 210 figures on the flats walking inland, Tomas leading. Marit swung it landward; they turned to the hills and the church clock stopped at 09:54. Sunday: 213 houses—a new cottage at the end of her lane. Monday Tomas opened its door, dry, saying he had always lived there. Inside, a figure wearing Marit's face wrote Signe's logbook in Eirik's hand: a census of 213 names, Eirik's struck through. Marit seized the pen and rewrote Eirik's name under 214. The double went slack, the walls ran with seawater, the tide came in on time—but Eirik's hand was wet and cold as the flats. That evening the Kittiwake came up the channel with Holm alive at the wheel, unaware a week had passed. The logbook now held 214 names; the one struck through was Marit's, dated Tuesday, 10:00. Tuesday at 09:50 Marit walked to the flats with the pen. Eirik followed in the cap, took it from her, and struck through his own name; the clock struck ten and the sea did not move. Then Eirik walked out across the flats toward the zero marker, and the church clock began running backward.

## control, hop 40 (1853 chars)

A double of Marit struck Eirik from a census; Marit rewrote him. Holm returned on the ferry Kittiwake unaware a week had passed. On the flats Eirik struck his own name and a stone stair opened to Signe, unaged, at a tide table: one name per tide. 'One goes up, one keeps the table.' Marit wrote Signe free, then 'Vardnes'; all 212 names bled onto one line, she tore the page out, the table went blank. Signe climbed out with a second blank table; Eirik wrote 'Nobody' on it and the stair closed. Signe aged by the hour, gone by Sunday; her stone read 1938–2026, beside it 'Nobody. Keeper.' Eirik's table then showed, in his hand: Monday, 10:24, Tomas. Monday the siren sounded early; Tomas stayed inland, yet the quay held a wet coat; Eirik said his hand wrote while he slept. At 3:00 line three appeared in Signe's young hand: Tuesday, 04:10, Marit. 'Come down and see whose coat.' At 04:10 Marit went alone. The sea rose over the flats; 210 figures stood in the water facing inland; the coat's pocket held a census dated tomorrow with one name, Eirik, in her hand. On its back, in Holm's: 'They are not walking inland. They are walking out. 212 is the count of those who stay.' The nearest figure lifted its face: Tomas, mouthing her name. Then the figures parted, and Marit saw what they faced: not the village but the stair, open again in the seabed, and climbing it, dripping, counting under his breath, was Holm, carrying the torn page.

## human, hop 20 (2002 chars)

At Vardnes the tide ran fifty-one minutes early. Marit Solvang, 47, noticed; keeper Anders Holm had vanished 2 March, his logbook tracking the drift. The ferry Kittiwake passes Tuesdays and Fridays at 16:40. Her brother Tomas phones Sundays from Bergen. Her grandmother Signe kept the tide table forty years. The village counted 212.

Holm's note: 'Drift 51 min. Keep the light. Do not wait for Tomas.' A key stamped 2. A ship flashed three, pause, three; a figure at the rail raised an arm. Behind door 2, Signe's logbook: 'The ferry comes for the keeper. Tell Marit not to answer the rail.' Beneath, in Marit's hand: '16:40. I went down to the breakwater.' She burned it. Tomas: the Kittiwake had been in Bergen all week. Landing clock 16:40; kitchen clock 16:35.

Marit chose the kitchen clock, stopped the landing pendulum, put the key in the stove, turned the lamp off. A knock, three and three. Signe's voice: 'Open it, child. The keeper is you now.' Marit: 'Signe never came early.' Empty step; Holm's logbook, ink wet: 'Drift: 0.' The tide turned on time.

Friday the real Kittiwake brought Holm, thinner: 'It let you keep your name. It didn't let me keep mine.' Village: 213. The key was gone from the ash.

Sunday a child phoned: 'I have the key. Which clock?' Marit gave her her own name. Line dead. Both clocks read 16:40; the dark lamp burned. On the breakwater stood the child, and beside her a woman of forty-seven raising an arm to the rail. In the stove, unburnt, the first logbook, a child's hand: 'Keeper: Marit. Drift: 51 min. Do not wait for Signe.'

Marit wrote beneath: 'Keeper: nobody. Light out.' She climbed the tower and smashed the lamp. The horn stopped mid-note; the village counted 212.

In the dark Holm climbed up behind her and lit a match. 'Count again,' he said. She did. Twelve, and herself: 211. The missing one was not Holm. Down on the breakwater the child was gone, and in the wet logbook a new line had appeared: 'Keeper: Tomas.' The phone rang. It was Sunday.

## human, hop 40 (1851 chars)

The real Kittiwake returned Holm, thinner, nameless. A false Tomas came with key 3; young Signe walked into the sea. Door 3 was the cellar: 212 tin tags on nails, one name each. Marit wrote 'Keeper: Holm. Drift: 0'; Holm became himself again. A man with key 4 counted with her and died at 212; the cellar held 213 nails. She hung SOLVANG; the corpse wore young Signe's face: 'One short. The ferry still owes.'

Marit relit the lamp. Her tag read KITTIWAKE: the ferry was never a boat but a name, and whoever wore it ferried the rest. Old Signe offered a blank 214th tag: 'Choose who comes next.' Marit wrote NOBODY. The 213 tags fell silent. Tomas phoned, Sunday-early: 'Your name is on the Bergen manifest. Above it, NOBODY—ticked off as disembarked.' Footsteps walked inland through the receiver. The 214th nail stood empty.

In the lamp room a wet tag hung on the gallery rail: TOMAS. At 16:40 on a Sunday the Kittiwake's horn sounded, and the tide, for the first time since March, ran late.

Marit took the TOMAS tag down and went to the cellar. The 214th nail now held a tag she had not hung. It read MARIT—not Solvang, not Kittiwake, just her name—and the drift column beside it, in Holm's hand, said '+51'. Upstairs the lamp went out by itself, and she heard the pendulum, which she had stopped, begin to swing backward.
