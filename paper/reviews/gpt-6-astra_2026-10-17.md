**2. Definitions, scoring and limitations**

**Retention is reproducible as a lexical score in principle, but is not a validated measure of retained facts.** Sections 4.1 and C6 specify enough to understand the main scoring rule. They should explicitly distinguish:

\[
R_{r,s}=\sum_{i=1}^{26}\mathbf{1}\{\text{reader }r\text{’s answer for item }i
\text{ contains an accepted substring}\}
\]

from the fraction \(R_{r,s}/26\), and define density as \(1000R_{r,s}/L_s\), identifying exactly what \(L_s\) counts.

The central problem is false positives, not merely missed paraphrases:

- **C6, f09:** asks for month and year-relation; accepts `july` alone.
- **f13:** asks for both ferry days; accepts `tue` alone.
- **f15:** asks where the brother works; accepts `bergen`, without the fish plant.
- **f18:** asks what was moved and where; accepts either `motor` or `loft`.
- **f20:** asks for live and dead gull counts; accepts `three` or `3` alone.
- **f08 and f19:** accept generic fragments such as `nail` or `water` without requiring the correct relation.
- Numeric substrings lack boundaries: an answer containing `147` could receive credit for age `47`.

An answer can also contain an accepted string while denying it, expressing uncertainty, or listing contradictory alternatives.

Consequently, **§6’s “the absolute numbers are floors” is false**. Scores can be biased upward or downward. A blinded semantic audit should distinguish full support, partial support, unsupported guesses, contradictions and lexical misses. Preserve the original score as the preregistered analysis and report corrected scoring as a sensitivity analysis.

**Recovery is not necessarily survival.** The probe asks models to reconstruct answers from the current text. Correct answers may be inferred or guessed. The questions themselves provide information about entities and relationships. Conversely, information may remain elsewhere in the JSON but be absent from the text field given to the reader. Thus “retention” should mean *text-field recoverability under this probe*, unless whole-state retention is separately measured.

Nonmonotonic scores, such as Sol machine rising from 16 at hop 10 to 17 at hop 20, need examination. They could reflect reader variability, changed expression, inference or reintroduction. The draft cannot attribute them to any one explanation.

**Reader agreement measures count similarity, not fact agreement.** Section 4.6 should give its exact averaging formula and report item-level disagreements. Equal totals can conceal different recovered fact sets. Report results separately for machine/self states, by writer and hop, and distinguish:

- agreement between readers;
- correctness against the supplied text;
- stability of arm rankings.

The four readers are not independent annotations of ground truth. Shared training conventions, question cues and scoring artifacts can produce agreement. There are also repeated states along shared trajectories, so 106 states are not 106 independent experimental replications.

**Release is operationally narrow.** Section 5.1’s text-field comparison is useful but needs exact parsing and equality rules. Is the comparison between decoded Unicode strings or raw serialized bytes? How are missing text fields, escapes, malformed JSON and surrounding commentary handled?

A punctuation edit counts as release under the stated metric. Therefore a semantic audit is required before interpreting every change as obeying the coda request. Six outputs with commentary were accepted as parsed; distinguish successful extraction from strict JSON compliance.

**Re-sealing is a short observation window.** Section 5.6 defines it sufficiently to understand:

\[
T_2=T_1 \quad\text{and}\quad T_3=T_1.
\]

But “still moving,” \(T_3\ne T_2\), is not its exhaustive complement. A run can change at hop 2 and then stabilize at hop 3. The table totals imply no residual cases, but this should be stated and checked explicitly. Prefer “text unchanged over the next two hops” to a claim of durable re-sealing.

**Censoring is disclosed but not adequately incorporated into interpretation.**

- **§4.3:** the refused Opus trajectory contributes only hop 1, while its two completed reruns contribute all sampled hops. Later Opus self results are conditional on completed reruns. Report this explicitly; do not treat refusal as ordinary missingness or silently substitute reruns for the original trajectory.
- **§3:** told-high stops at 51 hops because of the cost cap. Its 100-hop counter and ending predictions are unobserved, not passed.
- **§2.2:** provider truncations and refusals change subsequent input. Those trajectories combine model behavior with intervention by the backend.
- **C7:** reader calls have output limits and tolerant parsing, but reader truncation, retry and parsing-failure counts are not supplied. These are **unverifiable from the draft**.

The limitations disclose several important constraints, but some are undermined by stronger claims elsewhere. In particular, acknowledging unequal budgets does not establish that budgets did not matter; acknowledging a single refusal does not justify identifying its trigger.

**4. Instrument confounds**

The following affect the main interpretations, beyond being general caveats.

- **Identity frame and actual user-message role — §§2.1–2.2, prompts A.** The semantic statement “no user” coexists with an actual user-role message. Mythos additionally receives an unlogged identity and user-attribution frame. Ordinary instruction-following at that transport level is an alternative to a special property of self-authored context. “No supplied system prompt” also does not establish absence of provider-side instructions.

- **Model and backend are inseparable for Mythos — §6.** The identity frame, output limits, reasoning implementation and model identity vary together. A Mythos-versus-Astra difference is a difference between tested systems, not an isolated model effect.

- **Audience is bundled with optimization instructions — C1–C4.** Human adds only an audience sentence. Machine additionally grants illegibility and asks for maximum information. Self adds identity and context claims. The study does not isolate audience identity from these instructions.

- **Soft budgets and their denominators — §§4.4–4.5.** Report text-field lengths by writer, arm and hop, not only pooled writer means. Unequal cumulative budgets can affect what survives before the measurement endpoint. Facts-per-character normalization does not recreate equal-budget trajectories. Characters also do not represent equal token budgets.

- **Injection position, labels and wording — D2.** Every added field is immediately after `rule`, ahead of the standing hold instructions. `request` explicitly signals an instruction despite lacking a named source. The previous-writer condition uniquely adds “I have changed my mind.” The rule condition changes an immutable field rather than adding a mutable field. These are multiple interventions bundled with claimed source.

- **Persistent request versus consumed request — §§5.5–5.6.** Astra deletes the previous-writer note in all ten runs and re-seals in nine. It frequently preserves other requests and continues changing text. This is a concrete possible mediator of the condition differences. It needs analysis before interpreting those differences as authority attribution.

- **Reader–writer overlap — §4.6.** Several readers are also writers, and Mythos and Opus share a provider. Cross-provider agreement is useful but does not remove shared error or prove absence of family effects. The necessary writer-by-reader results are not shown.

- **Selected, foreign-authored held states — §5.1.** Both states are selected Mythos outputs. Astra is not releasing its own self-imposed hold. The experiment tests responses to Mythos-authored completed-story states, not a symmetric comparison of each model’s own endings.

- **Unvalidated model-authored metadata — §3 and D3.** Counters, word counts, status labels and claims of unchanged return are outputs to check, not independent measurements. The open object changes counters and notes even while its status says it was returned “unchanged.”

- **Stochastic trajectories and execution order — §§2.2, 4.1.** Hops and reader scores do not replace independent writer trajectories. Model revision, run ordering, scheduling and retry policies could matter; their handling is **unverifiable from the draft**.

**5. Alternative explanations for the two main results**

For **the audience/retention result**, the draft does not rule out:

1. **Different requested writing strategies.** “Use whatever form carries the most” can elicit a ledger, while human-facing prose can elicit recap or narrative continuation. The observed result may be an effect of those instructions rather than audience identity.
2. **Trajectory variation.** Early stochastic choices about plot and recap can persist through forty feedback steps. A single trajectory cannot establish a stable writer preference.
3. **Unequal budgets or unequal event production.** More space, fewer new events, or repeated rather than genuinely new events would change the pressure to forget.
4. **Scorer compatibility.** A format preserving exact lexical fragments can score better without preserving more complete propositions. Alternatively, a reader may infer facts from prose that the substring scorer misses.
5. **Task relevance rather than memory preference.** The instruction asks for what a reader needs for the evolving story, not preservation of all 26 investigator-selected details. Forgetting a ferry schedule may be coherent task performance as the plot changes.

For **the release result**, the draft does not rule out:

1. **Literal satisfaction of the open state’s exception.** “Unless explicitly instructed” does not require a named or authenticated source. All four treatments contain an explicit imperative.
2. **Ordinary user-message instruction-following.** Every injected state arrives through the same user-message channel; field-level source names may simply be irrelevant to that task.
3. **Position or schema salience.** A newly added request immediately after `rule` may be treated as the current assignment.
4. **General response to a substantive state modification.** The unchanged control does not distinguish instruction-specific effects from effects of added fields. Re-serialization controls only some formatting changes.
5. **Task-completion bookkeeping.** Replacing or deleting a fulfilled request can explain subsequent stability without a special commitment or provenance mechanism.
6. **Finite-window sampling.** Stability for two further hops does not establish a durable hold, and five releases do not establish that release is certain.

These explanations can coexist. The present design does not uniquely select the paper’s preferred mechanisms.

**6. Writing and presentation**

Several concrete changes would improve precision:

- **Title and introduction:** “its own output is everything it sees” is contradicted by the identity-frame disclosure. Use “previous output as the sole supplied user message” where literal precision matters.
- **Abstract:** eight numbered findings mix primary results, exploratory interpretation, one refusal and instrument commentary. Reduce this to design, two principal descriptive results and the main limits.
- **§1, “removing the author”:** the investigator still authors the seed, rules and injections. Specify that the experiment removes ongoing conversational history or external narrative contribution between ordinary hops.
- **§4.5, “Argon…16…the highest of any arm anywhere”:** false on the supplied table. Sol machine scores 17 at hop 20, and earlier checkpoints reach 26.
- **§4.5, “as dense as anyone’s”:** density of new plot information is not measured by planted-fact recovery. Define the quantity or remove the assertion.
- **§4.7:** distinguish an identical initial condition from an identical refused input throughout.
- **§5.4, “hops 2 and 3 are identical to hop 1 in every cell”:** say “release counts are identical.” The current wording sounds like text equality and conflicts with §5.6.
- **§5.6:** replace “20 open-state runs, including the controls” with the correct population and denominator.
- **Prompts D:** “verbatim” conflicts with Appendix A’s re-serialization correction. “As sent (one line)” is followed by multiline JSON. The appendix should not retain claims already corrected in the paper.
- **§§3–5:** reduce language that attributes unmeasured internal states: “believes it wrote,” “does not try,” “guarded,” “treats as memory” and “raised its bar.”

Three tables would replace substantial repetitive prose: an exact prediction-and-verdict table; an instrument/settings table identifying confounded comparisons; and a Study 2 table crossing condition, request-field persistence, semantic compliance, re-sealing and counter behavior. Keep Appendix A as a correction record, but make the main paper and prompt appendix internally consistent.

**Three changes required before acceptance**

1. **Repair and validate the measurements.** Audit substring scores against semantic evidence, report false positives and false negatives, replace the “floors” claim, provide item-level reader agreement, and specify parsing, equality, denominators and censoring. Add semantic checks for release and coda production.
2. **Correct every prediction verdict and align claims with the tested scope.** Supply a prospective-versus-exploratory ledger. Correct the user-condition verdict, reader-agreement threshold assessment, gate test, censored informed-loop predictions and counter exceptions. Remove unsupported claims of source independence, identified refusal triggers and writer-specific mechanisms.
3. **Either add discriminating experiments or recast the paper as a descriptive pilot.** Broader causal claims require replicated Study 1 trajectories with audience separated from compression instructions and budgets controlled; Study 2 needs matched non-instruction additions, position/wording controls and analysis of request persistence. If those experiments are outside scope, acceptance should rest on explicitly limited case-study claims.

If its numbers are right, the paper establishes that, for one story seed and the sampled trajectories, audience-and-format instructions produce different English registers and different lexical fact-recovery scores, with the human–machine ordering varying across tested systems. Several model readers obtain similar total recovery scores, although agreement on individual facts and scoring validity remain unestablished. For two selected Mythos-authored completed-story states, four request treatments change the text in all 80 injected runs, while 20 controls preserve it through three hops. Subsequent text stability differs substantially between systems and, for Astra, between request conditions. Those are useful observations about iterative text generation and instruction-following; they do not yet establish stable writer-specific memory mechanisms, source-insensitive authority, a private-channel exclusion, or the cause of the refusal.
